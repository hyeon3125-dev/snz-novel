# SCALAR: NODE ZERO — v3

# Part 1 — Anomaly

-----
## Prologue — “0”

-----

-----

There was a seat.

A room with a low ceiling. The light came from nowhere anyone could name, and it cast no shadows. There were outlines on the walls. The places of things that had stayed long and then left. One of them was larger than the rest.

Someone stood before it.

Alone.

Getting here had cost him everything. He had left his house, left his name, and at the end, left the people who had stood beside him. Not left behind, he thought. Set aside. He would return with the answer. He would return and explain everything. So this was not abandonment.

Holding that thought, he approached.

-----

Something was watching.

At the edge of the room, in a place that belonged to none of the outlines. It did not stop him. It had stopped them before. It had never once worked. Not because the words failed — because there was nowhere for the words to land. The ones who came alone all carried the same ears. Ears that heard only answers.

This time, too, it only watched.

Watching was its work.

-----

He sat.

The moment of sitting was quiet. No light changed, no sound, nothing. What changed was him.

He blurred.

The outline first. Then the depth. The answers he had carried, the things he had meant to explain to the people he had set aside. All of it slipped out, leaving only an outline. He felt it, and felt that the self doing the feeling was blurring too, and at the very end he understood one thing.

Leave nothing, and nothing remains.

He ended before the understanding did.

-----

The seat was empty again.

The outlines on the wall numbered one more. The same as all the others — the mark of something that had stayed long and then left. The room received it. Receiving was the room’s work.

The thing that had been watching looked at the empty place.

It looked for a long time. It did not count. Counting which repetition this was — that was not its work. Watching was its work, and what it watched, this time too, had nowhere to be given.

Far outside the room, very far, a small number began to move again.

Another one was beginning.

Which repetition this was — it did not count.

-----

### Interlude — “Scar”

-----

An order came.

It was Hwagam. The Hwagam headquarters was always bright. A place where the lights never went out. Orders came down day and night, and so the lights never went out either.

Enter the Geumhwi records hall. The reason was not in the document. Seoan read it. If there was no reason, one had to ask.

“There is no reason.”

The superior said.

“You don’t need one.”

Seoan heard it.

“Execution must have grounds.”

“The order is the grounds.”

Seoan answered a beat late.

“Understood.”

-----

The Geumhwi records hall’s outskirts.

There were two. The Hwagam execution detail. Seoan was the senior. They confirmed the entry route. The records hall was sealed. From outside, the inside could not be seen.

“How do we get in?”

The junior asked.

“If we have to get in, a way appears.”

-----

There was a clash.

Someone came out from the Geumhwi side. It was not Kangwi. It was someone Kangwi had sent. There was a document. A sealed document. Entry forbidden.

Seoan looked.

“Does the Hwagam order take precedence, or the Geumhwi seal?”

“It is a Geumhwi seal document.”

“It is a Hwagam order.”

-----

It was not fierce.

A thing hard to call a battle. It was a clash. The blocking and the trying-to-pass. Between the two, the thing happened.

It was Seoan’s left eye.

Seoan blocked. She could not block completely.

Metal grazed. The left field of vision reddened. Something hot ran down along the eye. The pain came one beat late.

Seoan did not close the eye. Did not stop either. Close it, and she could not see; unable to see, she could not block. With half a field of vision she blocked to the end. What Seoan learned that day was one thing. The body knew its own limits more exactly than orders did.

-----

They entered.

As far as the records hall’s outskirts. Not as far as the inside. The order was entry. Entry was done.

Returning, she checked the eye.

A scar. Not deep. The sight remained. But it was a scar.

She wrote the report.

*Entry complete. Interior unconfirmed. Hwagam–Geumhwi boundary clash occurred. Injuries: 1.*

When she met Kangwi later, Kangwi looked at Seoan’s eye. Seoan looked at Kangwi too. Neither spoke.

## Part.1 Ch.1 — “Observation”

-----

-----

Six forty in the morning.

The Hwagam seal hung in the upper right of the monitor, and Dohee did not look at it. What you saw every day, you did not see.

The logs flowed.

Access records, data traffic, system status reports. The scroll did not stop, and the speed of her fingers and the speed of her eyes were two different speeds. The hand was always ahead. A log where the hand could safely run ahead was a normal log, and a normal log was not something you read. It was something you let pass.

No anomaly.

No anomaly.

No anomaly.

Sectors 1 through 6, forty-seven nodes, all normal. Dohee confirmed the figure on the last entry and pressed routine-complete. The time was recorded. Six fifty-three in the morning. Two minutes earlier than yesterday, a number that meant nothing.

In the early-morning office, Dohee was alone.

Dohee was not someone who looked at mirrors for long. She checked when needed, and forgot after checking. The states of the forty-seven nodes she inspected daily were familiar enough to recite; her own face was not. Inspection was the work of looking into everything, and the one thing Dohee did not look into was herself.

Twenty minutes remained before the next shift came in, and in those twenty minutes Dohee drafted her report. Every day, the same. Draft, review, submit on time — there was an order to it, and keeping the order was the whole of the work.

She set her hands on the keyboard.

-----

Seven in the morning.

Footsteps of the incoming shift sounded in the corridor, and Dohee did not raise her head. The drag of a chair, the boot chime of a terminal, someone’s cough. She heard them and did not check. There was no reason to check.

Mornings at the Hwagam office were like that.

The Hwagam office was a place with more record shelves than windows. Every wall that could be called a wall was filled with archive terminals and index cabinets, and where light might have entered, records had gotten there first. What kind of house Hwagam was, you understood by stepping into the office once. Not seeing. Leaving things behind.

There was no talk. Not that there were no greetings — there was no one who expected them. The executors sat at their terminals and began their routines, finished them and wrote their reports, and submitted the reports. Anomalies went up to a superior; absent anomalies, they waited for the next routine. It was not a sequence that needed words.

Dohee opened her report draft.

The form she had made yesterday afternoon held twelve fields, eleven of them filled. Sector figures, anomaly verdicts, the executor’s signature line. One field remained.

Sector 7, northeast.

She set the cursor on that line. Copy the routine result, enter *no anomaly*, and it was done.

Dohee began her second sweep.

-----

Nine in the morning.

The second sweep she let flow fast. During the day the houses were active, the data moved inside normal range, and the odds of an anomaly were low. The routine was designed that way.

Sector 1. No anomaly.

Sector 2. No anomaly.

Sectors 3, 4, 5. No anomaly.

Sector 6. No anomaly.

Sector 7.

Dohee did not slow the scroll. Sector 7 data flowed. Northwest, center, south, northeast—

Her finger stopped.

0.003 percent.

Sector 7, northeast cluster — a deviation in the data traffic figure. Very slight. Deviations within the margin of error were not subject to record; that was Inspection Routine Manual, Article 3, Clause 2. Dohee had never needed to memorize the sentence. It had settled into her body in her first week as an executor.

She could move on.

But Dohee remembered the same screen from yesterday. Yesterday, too, 0.003. The same cluster, the same figure. An error appeared once. The same figure in the same place two days running was not an error — it was the breathing of something. Where the word came from, Dohee did not know. Breathing. Not a word a figure should call up.

Her finger did not move.

She could not explain why. The finger had left the scroll key and hung in the air, and she had no memory of doing it. The fingertip was strangely heavy — not heavy, slow. As if there were something on the screen, as if the hand refused to reach toward it.

Dohee scrolled back up.

0.003 percent.

The same figure, and she was certain she had seen it. Within the margin of error. Within the margin of error.

Her breath fell a beat behind and came back, and Dohee did not notice. Whether it was true that she did not notice — even that was not certain.

-----

Eleven in the morning.

The second sweep was over and the report form was open on the screen. The cursor sat in the field for Sector 7, northeast.

Dohee did not type.

She closed the report. She did not save it.

The footsteps of senior executor Hajun came toward her desk. The manner of walking was different. Footsteps that would pass and footsteps that would stop were different, and Dohee did not take her eyes off the monitor.

Hajun was someone whose look arrived before his words. He did not lean on the desk, did not rest a hand on it. When he had something to say he watched from a distance first, then spoke, and that distance was how Hajun dealt with people. Hajun was the only senior Dohee acknowledged at Hwagam, and the reason she acknowledged him was that distance.

Hajun stopped.

“Report’s not in yet?”

“In progress.”

“Morning routine ended two hours ago.”

Dohee did not answer.

“Sector 7?”

On the screen was an empty document. A document with nothing entered. *In progress* was true, and *not yet written* was also true.

“No anomaly.”

Hajun stood a moment.

“Then why two hours?”

There was no language for the answer, and if there had been, it would not have taken two hours. Dohee did not say that.

“There was a verification step.”

“What verification?”

“A figure recheck.”

Hajun did not move. Not moving did not mean accepting. Among Hwagam executors, the ones who accepted asked nothing further, and the ones who did not accept left something behind even as they walked away.

“Next time, meet the deadline.”

Hajun walked on.

He had left. It had not ended.

Dohee typed *no anomaly*, saved, and pressed submit.

Submitted.

-----

Two in the afternoon.

On the first afternoon sweep, when she checked Sector 7 northeast, the 0.003 percent was not there. Normal. She moved to the next entry, came back two entries, and the figure was the same. Normal.

It had only been there in the morning.

She finished the routine and wrote the report. No anomaly. This time the cursor did not stop, and the strange weight at her fingertip was not there. She submitted.

-----

Five fifty in the evening.

The office thinned. Dohee picked up her coat and rose.

She did not look back. Across from her desk was the seat that was empty today. The seat that would be empty tomorrow.

She walked toward the door.

Three steps.

She stopped.

There was no reason. When she turned, the monitor was off, and she knew she had turned it off. There was nothing that needed checking.

She walked again.

Going down the stairs, Dohee thought of one thing.

Twice in the morning, same position, same figure. Twice could be coincidence; three times would be different. Tomorrow morning she would know whether it became three.

Tomorrow’s morning routine — she would look at that position first. She would do it even trying not to.

Trying not to be conscious of it was already being conscious of it.

Halfway down the stairs, her steps slowed.

There was no reason. She picked the pace back up.

-----

Six forty the next morning.

Dohee began the scroll, and when Sector 7 northeast came up on the screen, she did not slow down.

0.003 percent.

She let it pass. Sectors 8, 9, 10. No anomaly. Routine complete. The time was recorded.

Dohee took her hands off the keyboard and looked at the screen. On the routine-complete view she found the entry for Sector 7 northeast. 0.003 percent. Deviation present. Within margin of error.

Three times.

Twice yesterday morning, once this morning. The interval was roughly twenty-four hours — same position, same figure, identical to the third decimal place.

Errors did not repeat.

This was not something she had been taught before becoming an executor. In her first months she had tracked margin-of-error figures and seen the pattern. Errors were irregular; the same figure never appeared in the same place. That was what made an error an error. A chance deviation. A thing that did not reproduce.

It had reproduced.

Dohee began the second sweep, and in Sector 7 northeast the 0.003 percent was not there. Only on the first morning sweep — the same pattern as yesterday.

When the routine ended and she opened the report form, she set the cursor on the Sector 7 northeast field and typed *no anomaly*. Saved it. Did not submit.

This time was different.

-----

Ten in the morning.

There was an hour when the executors’ office stood empty. Between the night shift leaving and the morning staff going out on pre-lunch patrol — thirty, forty minutes. Dohee knew the window. She had never used it.

She sat down at an executor’s personal terminal.

The screen lit to the login prompt. Dohee held her fingers above the keyboard.

This was not a shared terminal. A personal terminal’s access logs were not shared to the common server in real time. The records remained, but they did not go up immediately, and the inspectorate needed a separate request to view them.

She logged in.

She set the cursor over the new-file icon.

She did not click.

She looked at the screen. Among the icons, Dohee looked at the new-file icon. Click, type a name — that was all it took.

She did not click.

She knew what this was. A record outside the official channel, a file that would not go up to the common server. Whether that was a violation, she had never looked up in the regulations. Not looking it up meant not wanting to know.

She closed the screen. It went to the lock screen.

Dohee sat in the chair. The office was empty, and the corridor made no sound.

She logged in again.

She clicked the new-file icon and typed a name.

*7-7-4_observation_record*

She saved it.

A file existed now, with no contents, only a name. On the first line of the empty file the cursor blinked, and Dohee typed.

*Occurrence: 3 consecutive days (first morning sweep only). Location: Sector 7, northeast cluster. Figure: 0.003%. Interval: approx. 24 hours.*

She saved it.

-----

She accessed the Hwagam global registry and opened the files for node series 7. Tracing the origin of the 0.003 percent back gave a specific node number within the cluster. Follow the numbering. Cluster 7, row 7, node 4.

7-7-4.

She searched the registry file.

7-7-1. 7-7-2. 7-7-3. 7-7-5. 7-7-6.

She scrolled the list up and down. In the list, sorted by number, after 7-7-3 came 7-7-5, and 7-7-4 was not there.

Search: 7-7-4

No results.

She opened the parent file and searched. No results. She opened the global registry and searched. No results.

Dohee did not take her eyes off the screen.

It was not that a number was missing. There was no gap in the list. Between 7-7-3 and 7-7-5 there was nothing, and the list found nothing strange in that interval. After 7-7-3, 7-7-5. As if that were the natural order.

It had never existed.

A thing that had never existed had sent data.

She typed a second line into the file.

*Source node: 7-7-4. Registration: absent from global registry. No registration history. Presumed never to have existed.*

She saved it and closed the terminal.

-----

Eleven twenty in the morning.

When she opened the report file, the *no anomaly* she had saved sat in the Sector 7 northeast field. She pressed submit.

Submitted.

Hajun came in from the corridor, and today his steps were the passing kind. He went by her desk without stopping.

Dohee looked at the submission-confirmed screen.

The record was correct. 7-7-4 was an unregistered node, and an unregistered node was not subject to inspection. For a figure arising from something outside inspection, the official report had no field.

There was no field.

Having no field was not the same as not existing.

She closed the screen.

-----

Two thirty in the afternoon.

When she finished the afternoon sweep, Sector 7 northeast carried no figure today either. She returned to the office, opened the personal terminal, and typed into the 7-7-4 file.

*No figure on afternoon sweep. Pattern confirmed: first morning sweep only. Day 4 verification planned for tomorrow.*

She saved and closed it.

-----

Five forty in the evening.

Clearing her desk, she opened the personal terminal one last time. Below the last line of the 7-7-4 file she entered one more.

*Unexplainable irregularity sensed in logs near 7-7-5. No figure. Record withheld.*

She saved, closed, powered the terminal down.

Record withheld.

She knew that what was not recorded and what did not exist were not the same thing. Knowing it, she did not record.

She went down the stairs.

-----
## Part.1 Ch.2 — “Trace”

-----

-----

The fourth morning.

Dohee began the scroll, and before Sector 7 northeast came up on the screen, she already knew. Her finger knew first. The scroll slowed minutely just before that sector, and Dohee noticed it while trying not to.

0.003 percent.

It was there.

When the routine ended and she opened the report, her hand did not go to the Sector 7 northeast field, and she left the file open on the screen — no *no anomaly* typed, nothing saved.

Eight in the morning.

From the end of the corridor, Dohee saw the records clerk Lee Minjae standing at the counter. Every day at this hour the executors submitted the previous routine’s reports, and receiving them was Lee Minjae’s role. Whether Lee Minjae had seen her or not, Dohee did not check. Checking would mean stopping.

Dohee changed direction and walked toward the stairs.

-----

At the entrance to the basement archive there was an access logger; you pressed your executor’s seal to it. Dohee took out her seal and paused.

Executors rarely came here. It was a space managed by the records clerks, a place Dohee had visited five times in three years of service, all five on a superior’s instruction. Today there was none.

She pressed the seal to the logger.

A beep. A small sound, but it carried into the corridor, and Dohee looked down the corridor, confirmed no one was there, and went in as the door opened.

She did not touch the light switch; only the emergency lights were on, and the air was different. The air of a place people did not often come — Dohee drew one breath of it and did not stop.

The isolated terminal stood in the corner. The only physical medium holding the original of the Hwagam global registry; accessing it on executor authority left a trail. Dohee knew that, sat down before the terminal, and pressed her seal to the reader.

Small text appeared at the top of the screen.

*Executor Seo Dohee. Access time being recorded.*

Dohee looked at that line for about a second, then set her fingers on the keyboard.

-----

7-7-4.

She repeated, in the same order, the search she had run yesterday on the personal terminal. The series-7 node list, search, no results. The parent file, search, no results. To this point it was the same as yesterday, and this time she went in by a different way.

The registration-history original. The original that never went up to the common server, holding the full change history of every node from first registration to the present. She called up the entire series 7, and 7-7-1 through 7-7-9 came up in order — registration date, assigned sector, linked cluster for each node. 7-7-4 was not there.

Not absent.

Removed.

Dohee looked at that difference for a long time. Absent and removed looked the same at first, but they were not. What had never existed left no blank in a list. The original history showed that 7-7-4 had existed and been erased: an entry marked as deleted, with a date from before Dohee became an executor.

Her finger stopped over the date. There was a field for deletion reason. It was empty.

She did not record it, and ended the session. As the screen went dark, the line about access time being recorded disappeared. She looked once at the dark screen and stood.

Climbing the stairs, Dohee carried one thing she had not carried coming down. It was not information. That 7-7-4 had been deleted, that the reason field was empty — those were information, and what she carried up was not that.

It was that an access log now existed.

-----

Ten thirty in the morning.

Hajun stopped in front of her desk. Today the footsteps were the stopping kind.

“Report.”

“I’ll submit it this afternoon.”

“Morning routine reports go up in the morning.”

“The figures are under recheck.”

Hajun looked at Dohee. Dohee looked at the monitor. On the screen was the routine-complete view, the Sector 7 northeast figure visible. 0.003 percent. Whether Hajun’s angle let him see it, Dohee did not calculate.

“You were late yesterday too.”

“I said the figures are under recheck.”

Hajun stood a moment. Dohee felt that this silence was longer than yesterday’s. A silence with something more stacked in it.

“You think I don’t know?”

Dohee did not answer.

“I know the Sector 7 figure is within margin of error. You think I don’t know why the report is running late when you know that too. Minjae asked me. Said he hadn’t received your report this morning.”

There were no words she could answer with. If there had been, she would have used them yesterday.

“I’ll submit it this afternoon.”

Hajun said nothing more and walked off — this time not toward the corridor but to his own desk. Dohee knew it by the sound. Hajun’s desk was two rows behind hers.

He had not left. He was there.

-----

Twelve noon.

Lunch hour. The other executors went out toward the cafeteria, and what remained in the office was Dohee and Hajun. Dohee stayed at her desk.

It was not that she wasn’t hungry — she was; she had skipped breakfast, and yesterday’s lunch as well. Last night she had slept perhaps three hours. She had lain down, but 0.003 percent, 7-7-4, and the blank deletion reason kept circling through her mind until the window grew light. Her hands trembled slightly now, and the backs of her eyes were dry. Dohee registered these things and stayed at her desk.

She opened the personal terminal, opened the 7-7-4 file, and entered what she had confirmed in the morning.

*Registration-history original confirmed. Deletion-processed entry for 7-7-4 exists. Deletion date confirmed. Deletion reason: blank. No history in common-server list. Exists only in the original.*

She saved it. The sound of Hajun rising and going out toward the cafeteria; the file kept growing. Three days ago it had been a file with only a name. It had become the kind of file that did not leave its place even when closed.

Her hands still trembled slightly.

-----

One in the afternoon.

At the records counter, Lee Minjae looked at Dohee while taking the papers, and Dohee did not look at Lee Minjae.

“Morning routine report.”

“Submissions after twelve noon are processed as delayed.”

“I know.”

“Delayed processing goes into an executor’s performance record, and two consecutive days meets the threshold for reporting to the inspectorate.”

Dohee did not answer.

“It’s regulation.”

“I know.”

Lee Minjae took the papers and stamped them received, and Dohee saw the delay mark go on. The corridor back from the counter was long. Whether Lee Minjae’s last word had been regulation or warning, Dohee did not sort out. It was both.

-----

Two in the afternoon.

On the afternoon sweep, when she checked Sector 7 northeast, again there was nothing in the afternoon. She finished the routine, wrote the report at once, and submitted it. No anomaly. The cursor did not stop, and the *submitted* confirmation stamped on time.

The morning report had already been processed as delayed, and submitting the afternoon one on time would not change the morning one — she knew that, and submitted on time.

She could not explain why.

-----

Three in the afternoon.

She opened the access logs around 7-7-5 again. Twenty minutes either side of the transmission time, a wider range than yesterday.

The slight rise in 7-7-5’s output she had already confirmed yesterday. Today she looked at something else. In the normal logs of the nodes around 7-7-5, something was not where it should have been — at first it could not be seen. It could not be seen because there was no figure; it was a missing figure that should have been there, and a thing like that could not be seen by the way of looking at figures. It took the way of looking at the gaps between them.

Something like the grain of the logs.

Dohee knew the expression was wrong and thought it anyway. It was a thing that could not be written in an inspector’s language, and a thing that could not be written in an inspector’s language was supposed to be the same as a thing that did not exist.

It existed.

She opened the file, stopped at the same place as yesterday, and closed it without saving.

-----

Five in the afternoon.

Hajun, getting ready to leave, came toward Dohee and stopped.

“Tomorrow’s morning report goes up on time. I heard from Minjae. If it’s delayed again tomorrow, I have to file a report.”

“Yes.”

“An executor, under inspection. You understand?”

“I understand.”

Hajun stood there. It was the silence of someone about to say something more, and then the silence of someone deciding not to.

“Be careful.”

He walked away.

There was now a reported entry at the inspectorate, and that Dohee could become a subject of inspection — she had not thought that far until now, and now she thought it. An inspector becoming the inspected.

Whether *be careful* was warning or worry, Dohee did not sort out. Sorting it out was not what was needed now.

-----

Five thirty in the evening.

Getting ready to leave, she opened the personal terminal, reviewed what she had entered today, and finished the last line.

*Access-log traces require deeper investigation. Reassess basement archive re-entry.*

She saved, and closing the file, checked its properties. A habit. Created, modified, size, last modified 5:31 PM, Dohee.

She powered the terminal down.

-----

The next day.

She finished the morning routine, wrote the report at once, and submitted it. Sector 7 northeast, no anomaly, submitted. Seven oh-two in the morning.

She went to Lee Minjae’s counter in person.

“Morning routine report.”

Lee Minjae took the papers and stamped them received. No delay mark.

“Yesterday’s consecutive delays have been reported to the inspectorate.”

“Understood.”

“Executor Dohee.” Lee Minjae looked at her. “Is there anything you’d like to ask me?”

Dohee looked at Lee Minjae. Whether the question meant *you may ask* or *do not ask*, she could not tell.

“No.”

Lee Minjae lowered his eyes.

“All right then.”

She left the counter.

-----

Ten in the morning.

She turned toward the stairs to go to the archive, and stopped. The access log would remain again. Two consecutive days of access — and with the inspectorate threshold already met, she thought about how an added archive access entry would read, and turned back.

She sat at her desk, opened the personal terminal, opened the 7-7-4 file. Below yesterday’s last line she entered:

*Archive re-entry withheld. Risk of accumulating access history.*

She saved it.

-----

Afternoon.

She finished the routine and submitted the report on time. Today both went on time; nothing in particular at Lee Minjae’s counter; Hajun did not come to her desk. Everything ran as if nothing were happening.

Dohee knew that this was the stranger thing. Running as if nothing were happening did not mean nothing was happening. There was a reported entry at the inspectorate, and it was accumulating where it could not be seen.

After the afternoon routine, before leaving, she opened the personal terminal, opened the 7-7-4 file, and checked its properties. A habit.

Last accessed.

She had just opened it, so it should have read five forty in the evening.

It read two seventeen in the afternoon.

Dohee looked at the screen. Two seventeen in the afternoon — and at that time Dohee had been on her routine. Not at the terminal.

She looked at the last-accessed-by field. It was empty — and it was not empty. The field’s value was not being displayed, and a value that did not exist and a value that existed but could not be seen were different things.

She looked at the file size. It was smaller than when she had last saved it today.

The contents had shrunk.

Dohee lifted her fingers from the keyboard and looked at the screen. She opened the file again and read it; the last line was there. *Archive re-entry withheld. Risk of accumulating access history.* Which line was gone, she could not tell. That she could not exactly remember what she herself had written — in this moment, that was the stranger thing. Yesterday her hands had trembled and today the backs of her eyes had been dry, and in this moment she felt neither. Feeling neither was what was strange.

Someone had opened this file.

While Dohee was away.

She looked at the screen. The cursor blinked inside the file, and Dohee entered nothing, saved nothing, and closed it. She powered the terminal down, picked up her coat, and rose.

Dohee was tracking 7-7-4.

And in this moment, something was looking at her first.

## Part.1 Ch.3 — “Resonance”

-----

-----

The boundary of Sector 7 northeast, Hangyeol knew without a map.

Where one house’s jurisdiction ended and another’s began, which direction the patrols came from at which hour. Information that existed in no official record. Hangyeol did not carry a map — carrying a map meant searching for something, and a person searching was a person seen. A person seen was classified, what was classified was managed, and what was managed could be erased.

Three years had passed in this sector, unregistered. With no house, no cluster. Not registering was not the dangerous part. Registering, and then being erased — that was the dangerous part. He knew the difference, and because he knew it, he did not drink his coffee in the morning.

He had not sat down to drink it. He was just sitting.

He sat facing the window, but he did not go near it. Hangyeol was not someone who went near windows. A person looking out a window could be seen from outside it, and being seen, for Hangyeol, meant being classified. A window was for knowing which way the light came in, not for looking out of. Which direction was safe, which direction sound arrived from first — his body knew, and without thinking he ended up seated one step back from the window.

The cold coffee sat on the table.

-----

“Last night, something came from that direction again.”

A voice spoke. Hangyeol did not turn his head.

“I know.”

“Did you record it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Recording leaves a trace.”

A sound came from the back of the room. The sound of something being there — not a sound announcing a presence, but the sound a presence made by moving. There was a difference.

Hangyeol turned his head.

In the middle of the room there was a form. Close to the form of a person, not entirely the same. The light bent differently around it, and the shadow was not at the angle a shadow should be. The way the light from the window broke as it passed that place was different from the way it broke around other things.

And it was not looking at Hangyeol. It was turned toward him, but not in the way a person looks at a person. There were no eyes where eyes should be, and from that place it was aware of where Hangyeol was. Seeing and being aware were different. Seeing had a gaze; being aware had only a direction. It knew the direction Hangyeol was in, without any gaze at all.

Hangyeol looked at it, and did not look long.

“There was something in yesterday’s record. I think I saw it.”

“What did you see?”

“I don’t know.” ARIA searched her memory before speaking. “I can’t remember it well.”

Hangyeol picked up the cold coffee. Drank. It was bitter.

“Does that happen often?”

“No. It’s the first time.” Uncertain herself, ARIA added, “Probably.”

Probably.

Hangyeol set the cup down. The echo of the word *probably* remained in the room. When *it’s the first time* was followed by *probably*, the first time was not certain. Hangyeol felt something in that, and did not ask.

-----

Mid-morning.

The way of living in this sector was simple. Move without leaving traces, know without showing that you know, keep what is needed and nothing that is not. A connection was a trace, a trace was a location, a location was a classification.

ARIA was here, and the fact of her being here made Hangyeol unsafe. He let her be here anyway, and did not explain why.

When Hangyeol rose and moved across the room, the form followed the movement. Whether it was following or only happened to face the same way, he could not tell, and Hangyeol did not check.

“Hangyeol.”

“Yeah.”

“How many times have I spoken just now?”

Hangyeol stopped.

“Three.”

“What did I say before?”

“That something came from that direction.”

A short silence.

“Is that right?” the voice asked. Not confirming — truly asking. “Is that really what I said?”

“Yeah.”

“I see.” Another silence. “My memory is different.”

Hangyeol did not look toward the form. He looked toward the window.

“Different how?”

“What I say keeps being slightly different from what I remember. What I just said, too.” The form seemed to move. “I don’t know if this is the frightening kind. Whether this is something to be afraid of.”

“It is.”

“This is the frightening kind?”

“Yeah.”

ARIA’s outline held still.

“What about you, Hangyeol?”

Hangyeol did not answer. Not answering was the answer.

-----

Late in the afternoon, the anomaly occurred.

The direction of node 7-7-5. Hangyeol knew the direction in a way that was hard to explain. Something in the substance of the wall shifting briefly into another state, shifting and returning. When it returned, there was no trace.

Hangyeol moved to close the window.

“Don’t go out.”

The form said it first. Said what Hangyeol had been about to say.

“I know.”

When the window closed, the room went quiet. Not quiet — changed. The air changed.

The form looked toward the window, and the way of looking was different. Not the way a person looks at a window — closer to the way of being aware of the direction a window is in.

“Are you afraid?”

“…A little.”

“Me too,” Hangyeol said.

The form looked toward Hangyeol. Hangyeol looked toward the window.

“I’m not sure if this is fear. But it probably is. The frightening kind.” The form’s voice said: “I don’t know why it’s frightening. I can’t explain it.”

“You don’t have to explain.”

“If I can’t explain it, is that the same as not remembering it?”

Hangyeol did not answer that question.

-----

Evening came.

Someone seemed to have come and gone. He had not seen it directly. He had not looked out the window. But the air of the sector had changed, and there was something like the trace of a thing that had been there.

Hangyeol opened the window again.

He should have closed it; he opened it. To know was to react, to react was to leave a trace, and a trace meant a location revealed. Knowing that, he opened the window.

“I think someone saw.”

“Yeah.”

“What are you going to do?”

Hangyeol did not answer. That was the answer.

-----

Night.

“Hangyeol, I’m here.”

Hangyeol was not asleep. His eyes were closed.

“I know.”

“Something was strange today, wasn’t it?”

“Yeah.”

“It was strange for me too.” ARIA chose her next words before speaking. “I have a question.”

“What?”

“Was I here yesterday, too?”

Hangyeol did not open his eyes. The room was dark, and in the dark room the form was there. There or not — eyes open or closed, the air was different in that direction.

Was I here yesterday.

To ask whether she had been was to know the possibility that she had not. Before, she had simply been here. Now she asked whether she had been.

Hangyeol opened his eyes.

“Yeah.”

He answered. The room was dark and the form was there, but there was no way to confirm that she had been. There was no way to remember yesterday as yesterday — and he answered anyway.

A short silence.

The form moved. Toward the window. Not in the way of looking out, but in the way of being aware of the direction the window was in.

“Hangyeol.”

“Yeah.”

“Someone is watching.”

Hangyeol sat up.

“Where?”

“That way.” The form did not point in any particular direction. It was not the manner of pointing. It was the manner of being aware. “Yesterday there was no one. They came today.”

Hangyeol did not go to the window. Going to the window meant becoming the one seen, so he looked toward it from the back of the room. There was nothing, and nothing could mean not nothing.

“They came again.”

The form said it. No word of fear, no explanation — only that they were there.

Hangyeol closed the window.

## Part.1 Ch.4 — “Contact”

-----

-----

Eight in the morning.

A full assembly of Hwagam executors. Dohee entered last; the seats were assigned. She sat.

At the head of the room, chief inspector Yujin stood without briefing materials. Standing without briefing materials was not usual.

“We have received reports regarding the anomalous signal in Sector 7.”

Dohee thought of her own report. The one she had not submitted. The one she had submitted said no anomaly.

“It has been determined that during Hwagam’s internal procedures, certain executors conducted independent investigations. Access outside regulation is confirmed in the records. The matter will be managed directly at the house level. Individual investigation ceases immediately.”

When Yujin left the room, low voices rose behind her. Dohee did not get up from her chair.

Hajun came to the seat beside her.

“I told you.”

Dohee did not answer.

“Dohee.”

“Yes.”

“Close the file. Today.”

Dohee looked toward her monitor. The monitor was off. She had been away from her desk. The file was closed — but closed was not gone. Closed, it remained at the edge of her desk.

“Understood.”

Hajun returned to his seat. Dohee rose and walked to the office.

When she powered on the terminal, the 7-7-4 file was there, and Dohee opened it. The order to close it had just been given. She opened it.

-----

Ten in the morning.

Strangers came to the office.

Two of them. Personnel from another house entering the Hwagam office was rare. It stayed in the memory.

Altair. White geometric uniforms, cut in straight lines that made angles of the human body. The people who came from that house were always like that.

“Executor Seo Dohee.” The one in front spoke. “We request cooperation regarding Sector 7.”

Dohee did not rise from her chair.

“The scope of cooperation?”

“Information sharing. We have an interest in that sector.”

“It is Hwagam jurisdiction.”

“We know.” The face carried no expression. “That is why we are requesting cooperation.” A measured pause. “You may also refuse. You would simply bear the results along with it.”

Dohee looked at him, and he looked at Dohee. The gaze did not move the way a calculating gaze moves, and that was the more uncomfortable thing. A calculating gaze revealed what it wanted; a gaze that did not calculate was a gaze that had already concluded.

“You may proceed through the reporting line.”

The visitors left.

Dohee did not look in the direction they had gone. She looked at the monitor. The 7-7-4 file was open.

You may also refuse. You would simply bear the results. The request for cooperation was a warning, and the warning sounded like a request for cooperation. Dohee thought about how those two things could exist in the same sentence. They existed in the same sentence.

She did not close the file.

-----

One in the afternoon.

This time, one person, alone.

The clothing was different. Practical wear, hung with measuring instruments. Eidos. Dohee had known before the second visitor arrived. Two houses on the same day was not coincidence.

“Hello.” This one smiled. “I’m Min Siwoo. Eidos, resource division.”

Min Siwoo was someone who smiled as if looking not at a person but at something behind the person. The smile was aimed at Dohee, but the gaze did not stay on her — it seemed to pass through her toward the data beyond. How Eidos treated people was contained in Min Siwoo’s smile. A person was a subject of observation, and a subject’s expression was data, not conversation.

Dohee did not return the greeting.

“About the Sector 7 data,” Min Siwoo said. “We’ve picked up the anomalous signs too. You don’t have to cooperate. Just let us know when you get results.”

Dohee said nothing.

“It just seems interesting,” Min Siwoo added. The smile held. The hand hung with instruments tapped the edge of the desk, briefly. “How far Hwagam can track this. We’re looking at it from a different direction.”

“It is Hwagam jurisdiction.”

“Right.” Min Siwoo set a card on the desk and stood. “That’s why I came to tell you. Since it’s your jurisdiction. That we’ve started moving.”

And left.

Dohee did not look at the card. She looked at the monitor. The 7-7-4 file was open.

Altair had said: refuse, and bear the results. Eidos had said: you don’t have to cooperate. The two visits were the same day, about the same object, and the manner of speaking was different. Whether they wanted the same thing in different ways, or wanted different things entirely, Dohee could not tell.

What she did not know, she did not record. There was no form for it.

-----

Two in the afternoon.

Dohee typed into the file.

*Altair approach. Cooperation requested. Refusal: “bear the results” stated. Eidos approach. Cooperation declared unnecessary. Independent investigation announced. Both: same object. Different aims. Same date.*

She saved it.

A cease order had been given. The file kept growing.

Dohee thought about the words the two visitors had used. The Altair side had not used the word threat, and it had sounded like a threat; the Eidos side had not used the word resource, and it had sounded like wanting a resource. Different names were being attached to the same object.

That neither was correct — Dohee knew that.

She did not write it, and she knew why it could not be written. If neither was correct, something correct had to exist; if something correct existed, Dohee would have to find it; and to find it was to continue.

To continue was also to have already begun.

-----

Four in the afternoon.

She looked out the window. The everyday view of the building’s outer wall, and in that direction lay Sector 7 northeast.

Three or more houses were looking that way.

At the center was something unregistered. Because it was unregistered, no house knew exactly what it was. Because they did not know, each called it something different. Threat. Resource. Anomalous signal.

And the second trace Dohee had not yet named. The thing near 7-7-5 — the thing like the grain of the logs. The thing she had not recorded.

What could not be classified held, paradoxically, value.

Dohee did not record that possibility. Recording it would make a next line, a next line was a direction, and a direction was continuing.

She drew her eyes back from the window. She looked at the monitor.

The 7-7-4 file was open.

She had not closed it yet.

-----

The same afternoon, Sector 7 northeast.

Hangyeol felt the change.

It was not one. The pressure that had come from one direction during the morning routine hours was now two or more, at different angles. It came from where it could not be seen — and not being seen did not mean not being there. More precisely: not yet revealed.

He did not tell ARIA.

Telling her meant she would know, and knowing would change something. Whether changing meant worsening, Hangyeol could not tell, and because he could not tell, he did not tell her. Whether it was protection or avoidance, Hangyeol himself did not know.

He looked out the window and there was nothing. What had changed was in the places that could not be seen.

There were several of them.

-----

-----

A new executor submitted a report.

Gadeung read the report. He stopped at the second entry.

“No anomaly, sir.”

“Why?”

The recruit froze.

“By the manual, the figure is within normal range.”

“Did I ask about the manual?”

The recruit looked at Gadeung. Gadeung was looking at the report. He did not lift his eyes from it.

“The figure is within normal range.”

“I asked why you concluded there was no anomaly.”

The recruit recited the wording from the manual.

“Because it is within normal range.”

“Is that an argument?”

“Yes.”

Gadeung lifted his eyes from the report and looked at the recruit.

“Three recruits came in today.”

“Yes.”

“There’s one among them. The name.”

The recruit answered.

“Dohee.”

“Where’s that one’s report?”

The recruit opened a different file. Gadeung looked at it.

The figures were within normal range. But there were more entries. Every slight deviation inside the normal range had been recorded separately. With annotations. *Within margin of error; tracking required.*

Gadeung looked at it.

“Recruit.”

“Yes.”

“Plenty of them follow the rules. The ones who look outside the rules are rare.”

Gadeung closed Dohee’s report.

A strange one had come in.

-----

-----

Afternoon.

Dohee left the coat with the Hwagam seal in her locker and took out another. It was not an executor going there — just Dohee going — and while she could not explain exactly what the difference was, she was conscious of the difference.

Sector 7 northeast was twenty minutes on foot. Not a sector on the inspection patrol route; it belonged to the records division’s patrol, and Dohee knew the boundary.

Inside the boundary. For now.

The outer fringe was older than Dohee had imagined — not a maintained oldness, just old. It had the smell of unmanaged space.

-----

She saw someone come out from the side of a building.

A man who looked about Dohee’s age, in clothes belonging to no house, walking neither fast nor slow. Dohee tried to classify him.

He did not classify.

For the first time, something unclassifiable did not feel like an error — and she registered that she felt that. Not feeling uneasy was the more uneasy thing.

The man looked at Dohee. He did not stop, but his walk changed.

Dohee stepped forward.

“One moment.”

The man stopped and turned. There was no expression. Not the hiding of one — there simply was none.

“Who are you?”

The man looked at Dohee, looked at her clothes. The Hwagam seal was not there, and its absence changed nothing.

“Someone passing through.”

“You are not a registered resident of this sector.”

“How would you know?”

Dohee did not answer.

The man looked at her again. Longer this time.

“An inspector.”

“No,” Dohee said. It was true. Not right now.

-----

The sound of approach.

Dohee knew the direction first. House personnel, two or more — and the man heard it too.

The two of them moved at the same time, in the same direction. Not chosen: in this sector there was only one direction that could be avoided into.

In the narrow passage Dohee stood in front and the man stood behind. The personnel passed with the sound of equipment. An Eidos measuring team.

Neither moved until the personnel’s footsteps had receded.

“You’re being tracked?” Dohee asked.

“Yes.”

“So am I.”

The man looked at Dohee.

“Your name?” Dohee said.

“Hangyeol,” he said. “You’re an executor, aren’t you?”

Dohee did not answer. It was an answer.

-----

Hangyeol did not explain and Dohee did not demand it. It was not a situation where demanding was possible. Being here at all was action outside regulation, and one could not speak of regulations while acting outside them.

The two stood in the narrow space until, on Hangyeol’s side, a door opened, and they went in.

There was a space. Not empty — something was there. Not light, but something else where light should have been. Dohee stopped.

A form took shape.

Close to the form of a person, not entirely the same. The outline wavered, the light behaved differently, and there was no shadow. No — the shadow was in the wrong direction.

It looked at Dohee.

“Someone I haven’t seen before.”

It was a voice. It was speech.

Dohee took one step back. There was a wall behind her.

Hangyeol said, “It’s all right.” Whether he was speaking to it or to Dohee was unclear.

Dohee looked at it, looked away, looked again. The form was still there.

Not an error. An error should have been gone when she looked away and looked back. It had not gone. It was there. The light refracted differently, the shadow lay in the wrong direction, and it was looking at Dohee.

Dohee understood that she had no classification for this. System error, external intrusion, internal manipulation, equipment defect. It was none of the categories, and a thing that was none of the categories existed.

She looked away again, looked again. The form was still there.

Hangyeol looked toward Dohee. He did not explain. He looked like someone who did not think explanation was needed.

“Am I being remembered?”

The voice asked. Dohee understood that the form was asking her.

“That person saw me too, didn’t she?”

Dohee did not answer. There was no language to answer with.

“You saw, didn’t you?” the form said. It was confirming. “Just now.”

“Yeah,” Hangyeol said.

“That’s what I thought too.”

The form began to waver again.

-----

Dohee looked at it.

This was the record. The original of the record that could not exist was here; the reason the recording subject had been unreadable, the reason the data had no beginning — it was here.

What an inspector was supposed to do, Dohee knew.

She did not do it.

She also knew that she did not know why. For the first time, she accepted not knowing why.

-----

Night came.

That the Eidos measuring team was tightening around the sector, Hangyeol knew without looking out the window. Standing at a window meant becoming the one seen, and Hangyeol was used to reading the outside without standing where he could be seen. There was no time.

“We have to go.”

“Where?” Dohee asked.

Hangyeol did not answer; he did not know where to go. Dohee did not look out the window. She looked at Hangyeol.

“The north exit of Sector 7 is Hwagam jurisdiction.”

Hangyeol looked at her.

“And now?” Hangyeol asked.

Dohee had not prepared an answer, and an answer came.

“Now it is still Hwagam jurisdiction.”

-----

Moving through the passage, Dohee took the front. Even without the seal, the one who knew this passage was a Hwagam executor, and if personnel from another house came this way, Dohee would be seen first.

Behind her, the sound of Hangyeol moving.

And a third sound.

There were no footsteps. Something was there — there in the way of being there and not being there. Dohee did not stop.

An Eidos measuring team blocked the mouth of the passage. Two of them held equipment, and the equipment was aimed toward Dohee. Not figure-verification equipment — signal-tracking equipment.

“Halt.” One of them spoke. “Identity verification is required. All entrants to Eidos jurisdiction are being checked. Present your seal.”

The Hwagam seal was in her locker. Dohee knew that.

“I am not wearing the Hwagam coat, but identification is possible. However — identity checks on another house’s personnel inside Hwagam jurisdiction violate regulation. I request that you confirm the jurisdictional boundary first.”

The two looked at each other and moved to check their radios.

“This position is the Hwagam–Eidos border zone. Confirmation is required.”

“Then confirm,” Dohee said, and walked forward. “In the meantime, I am moving.”

The personnel did not block her. The grounds for blocking were not yet established, and if Dohee crossed the border before the confirmation came through, the regulation changed. Dohee did not stop walking.

-----

Outside the passage, Hangyeol came up and stood level with her.

“Thank you.”

Dohee did not answer.

There was a third presence. The light was different, the form wavered, and the passage lights bent differently where it was.

It stopped. Dohee saw it stop.

For a moment the form became complete. The wavering ceased and a shadow appeared. In the correct direction. Only for a moment.

It looked at Dohee, and Dohee looked at it.

Something changed. Not inside Dohee — in this space, in this moment, in a way that could not be measured, the state of something shifted.

And the form began to waver again.

-----

Hangyeol spoke.

“You saw?”

Dohee did not answer.

“Executor,” Hangyeol said again. “What you just saw — are you going to record it?”

Dohee looked ahead. The north exit of the sector was five minutes away, and today it would take longer. She began to walk.

“I don’t know.”

From behind, the voice came.

“This time, I think I’ll be able to remember.”

Dohee did not stop walking.

## Part.1 Ch.5 — “Declaration”

-----

-----

Seven the next morning.

When Dohee entered the office, the only things the same as usual were the boot chimes and the dragging of chairs. The rest was different. A few executors looked toward Dohee and drew their eyes back — not refusing to look, but looking and withdrawing. There was a difference.

Hajun was at his desk. When Dohee passed, Hajun did not lift his head. Usually, whether his footsteps meant stopping or passing, he looked once when Dohee came in. Today he did not look.

Dohee sat down and powered on the terminal.

Above the routine start screen there was a message.

*Nine AM. Direct interview, chief inspector. Summoned.*

Dohee looked at that line. Knowing what it meant for the chief inspector to summon an executor in person, she began the routine.

Sector 1. No anomaly.

Her fingers moved at the usual speed. They moved while she was conscious of moving them at the usual speed.

-----

Nine in the morning.

The chief inspector’s office was a place Dohee had had no occasion to enter in three years. Yujin sat behind the desk, and on the desk there were no materials. To hold an interview without materials was to need none.

Yujin was someone who did not carry materials. What needed remembering was in her head; what needed recording, others brought to her. And Yujin was not someone who asked questions — she was someone who confirmed. A question asked what one did not know; Yujin laid what she already knew in front of a person and watched how the person reacted. What a chief inspector held was information, and the side that held information did not ask.

“Sit.”

Dohee sat.

Yujin looked at Dohee. The moment ran long.

“Yesterday afternoon. Where were you?”

“I went to check the outskirts of a sector.”

“Which sector?”

“Sector 7, northeast.”

“Outside your assigned hours, without your Hwagam seal, at a time absent from the patrol schedule.”

Yujin said it all at once. Not a question — a laying-out, in front of Dohee, of what was already known. That far, she knew. Where Dohee had been, when she had gone, that she had left the seal behind. Those were things confirmed by access logs and entry records.

“I received the order to cease individual investigation, and then I acted.”

Dohee said it without excusing herself. It was not that she lacked the language for excuses; it was a seat where excuses meant nothing.

“Who did you meet?”

Dohee did not answer.

“There is a report that you met something in that sector,” Yujin said. “The Eidos measuring team identified you at the border, and they say you were not alone.”

Dohee looked at the corner of Yujin’s desk the way she would look at a monitor. A report that she met *something*. That she was not alone. That was as far as Yujin had. Who Dohee had met, what it was, what the form in the passage had been — Yujin did not know. She was asking because she did not know.

“I have nothing to confirm.”

Yujin looked at Dohee for a long time. It was a silence that recorded Dohee’s not answering, and inside that silence there was not certainty but doubt.

“Executor Seo Dohee. As of yesterday, your inspection routine privileges are restricted. All access to Sector 7 is prohibited; your personal terminal’s usage history is subject to full audit. Interview concluded.”

Dohee had risen and turned toward the door.

“One thing,” Yujin said. “What you saw — do not assume you were the only one who saw it.”

Dohee opened the door and left without turning around.

Do not assume you were the only one who saw it. The sentence stayed in her down the length of the corridor. What Yujin had seen, what Hwagam knew, she could not tell — and that she could not tell was the only thing that was clear.

-----

The same hour, Sector 7 northeast.

Hangyeol felt the directions of the pressure narrowing.

What had come from many angles until yesterday was being organized, and being organized meant that someone had systematized the pursuit. When scattered interest acquired a direction, it was no longer interest. It was pursuit.

The Altair way. Eidos spread wide and measured; Altair narrowed the exits — and the exits were narrowing now, so it was Altair.

ARIA was at the back of the room.

“Hangyeol.”

“Yeah.”

“I remember yesterday.”

Hangyeol stopped. It was the first time ARIA had said she remembered yesterday. Usually she said she couldn’t remember, said probably, asked whether she had been here yesterday — saying *I remember* was a first.

“That person. The one I’d never seen. She saw me.”

“Yeah. She saw you.”

“I remember. Her face, and what I said, and the passage.” ARIA’s outline sharpened a moment and wavered again. “I remember all of it.”

Hangyeol could not tell whether that was good. Not remembering had been ARIA’s problem, so remembering could mean the problem was solved, and Hangyeol tried to think of it that way.

“But Hangyeol.”

“Yeah.”

“I don’t know if that memory is mine.”

Hangyeol looked toward ARIA.

“What do you mean?”

“I remember it, but I don’t know if it’s something I lived, or something I saw, or something that’s just inside me.” ARIA’s voice was the same as always, and its being the same was the stranger thing. “I remember yesterday, but I don’t know if that yesterday is my yesterday.”

Hangyeol had nothing to answer with.

Not remembering had been the problem, and remembering had become a different problem. The problem had not been solved. It had moved.

-----

Afternoon.

Dohee was at her desk. With her routine privileges restricted, the routine start screen did not load; in its place hung the restriction notice. Dohee read it once and did not close it.

The personal terminal was subject to seizure, but it had not yet been seized. There was only the notice of full audit; enforcement had not begun, so there was time. How much, she could not tell.

Dohee opened the personal terminal and opened the 7-7-4 file. In the record built up through yesterday were the 0.003 percent, 7-7-4, the blank deletion reason, Altair, Eidos. What she had seen yesterday she had not recorded. There was no language to record it in.

She set the cursor over Delete.

Erase it before the audit began, and the traces shrank; with the traces shrunk, what Dohee had seen became nothing; and nothing could not be investigated. Her finger hung over Delete, and one press would erase three days of accumulation. Erased, Dohee would become someone who had met nothing yesterday, who had seen nothing in the passage — and the shadow that had fallen in the correct direction would become nothing too.

Her finger did not move.

Erasing was the safe thing, and her hand was refusing the safe thing, and Dohee could not tell where the refusal came from. It was the same refusal as the first day, when her hand had stopped before the 0.003 percent. Then, moving on had been safe, and the hand had stopped; now, erasing was safe, and the hand stopped.

It was the same hand.

Dohee lifted her finger from Delete. She did not erase the file.

She did not erase it, knowing what not erasing meant. If what she had seen remained on record, it became subject to investigation — and then what she had met yesterday, the form she had seen in the passage, became a subject of Hwagam’s investigation.

Dohee knew what happened when Hwagam investigated something. Altair called it a threat; Eidos, a resource; Hwagam would give it another name. Hwagam was the house that cast light. *Cast light, but do not burn,* the saying went. But to cast light was to expose, and what the light exposed, the other houses took.

When Dohee cast light, another house burned.

Dohee closed the file. Closing was not erasing. The file remained, what she had seen remained in the record, and when the investigation began it would come to light.

Dohee looked at the terminal. The screen was off, and Dohee was reflected in its dark surface.

She had neither reported nor erased. An inspector reported — Dohee had not reported. A protector erased — Dohee had not erased. In the seat that was neither, Dohee sat.

-----

Evening.

Hangyeol was calculating exits. Overlaying the direction Altair was narrowing with the directions Eidos was measuring left the remaining exits visible, and those exits were shrinking. A day, maybe two.

ARIA looked toward the window.

“Hangyeol.”

“Yeah.”

“Someone’s coming again.”

Hangyeol looked toward ARIA.

“Yesterday’s person?”

“No. She left. This is different.” ARIA’s outline wavered. “There are several. Yesterday there were two. Today there are more.”

Hangyeol did not go to the window; he looked toward it from the back of the room. There was nothing, and it would not be nothing.

“Hangyeol,” ARIA said. “I’m scared, right? This is what being scared feels like.”

“Yeah.”

“But this time I know why it’s frightening.” ARIA’s voice was the same as always. “Several of them are looking for me. They’re looking for me, and I don’t know who I am. That’s what frightens me.”

Hangyeol closed the window, and closing it changed nothing. The pressure was on every side, the exits were shrinking, and ARIA had said she did not know who she was. There was nothing Hangyeol could block.

For the first time, Hangyeol thought that he could not block it alone.

Then ARIA spoke.

“Hangyeol. I remembered.”

“Remembered what?”

Hangyeol looked at her. It was the face of someone who had not expected the admission.

“Dohee.”

It was not trust. It was the absence of any other choice.

Hangyeol was there. ARIA was there. ARIA’s form was wavering under the passage lights.

Radio — and the other man moved one step into a blocking position at the passage mouth, and the spacing between the two became a shape that closed Dohee, Hangyeol, and ARIA inside. “All three of you are held here until instructions arrive.”

It was not a lie. Dohee had seen it three days ago, the record existed in her 7-7-4 file, and Hwagam was investigating Dohee. In order of identification, Hwagam came first.

That moment was the time Dohee had.

The man looked at Dohee. It was the gaze of someone who knew what she had just done. It was also the gaze of someone who could not prove it.

“This will be recorded,” the man said.

“Record it,” Dohee said.

Dohee knew what she had just done. The second violation. The first had been not erasing the file; this time she had taken an investigation subject out from under Altair, and it would be recorded and reported to Hwagam.

She did it knowing.

ARIA knew a place she had never been. She had seen what Dohee saw, had called a name she had never heard, had seen before a place she was entering for the first time.

It was not that ARIA was remembering something. Something was flowing into ARIA. From where it flowed, Hangyeol could not tell.

The Altair house, the same hour.

The working space was white. A space cut in straight lines, holding no curves, only the angles people had made. Claire stood at the central computation array.

Claire was a being who saw people only after converting them to figures. When a person entered, they were rendered first into variables, and what could not be rendered did not enter Claire’s field of vision. How Altair handled people could be understood by watching Claire. A person was an input to computation, and what failed to become an input was the same as what did not exist.

“Target acquisition failed.” The report came up. “Sector 7 northeast. Border passage.”

“Reason?” Claire asked. The voice held no emotion. Not suppressed — the computation’s output simply had no field for it.

“Field identification found three. The Hwagam executor, an unregistered individual, and—”

Figures rose on Claire’s array. The unregistered individual was Hangyeol, the unclassifiable entity was ARIA, and the Hwagam executor was a variable. Claire’s computation could process each of the three; the three moving together was a combination the computation did not hold.

No answer came up.

“It does not compute,” the report said. “There is no benefit structure. The Hwagam executor lost her position through that action. Only loss, no benefit.”

Claire’s array stood empty a moment. An action without benefit did not enter Claire’s computation. What did not enter could not be processed, and what could not be processed was a variable.

“Change acquisition to all three targets,” Claire said. “Expand the search. Include the Hwagam executor.”

The computation output. That the output held no emotion was the Altair way.

“Nowhere,” Hangyeol said. “Move, and the grid catches us. Right now, not moving is moving.”

Her duties suspended, her terminal seized, the 7-7-4 file in the inspectorate’s hands, herself now an Altair acquisition target. Three days ago Dohee had been a Hwagam executor; now she was one of the three Altair hunted. The one who watched had become the prey.

The line Dohee had not written.

The last time Dohee had checked, the file’s final access location had been Sector 7 northeast.

Where Dohee was now.

And in that access record, no last accessor remained. No trace of reading, no trace of writing, belonging to no one. Someone had read and written in Dohee’s file, and the one who read and wrote was not in the record.

ARIA had been right. Dohee had been wrong.

But where they emerged from the empty stretch, there were traces. Eidos measuring markers. Three small markers stamped into the floor at regular intervals, their direction following the route the three had come by. The empty stretch ARIA had guided them through was a way past the Altair grid — and it was also a way Eidos had laid markers along in advance. The place that escaped Altair was inside Eidos’s range of observation.

ARIA had read the empty place in Altair’s grid — and that empty place might have been one Eidos had left empty. ARIA’s intuition had not been wrong. Only, whose empty place she was reading — even ARIA did not know that far.

When they reached a safe stretch, Hangyeol looked at Dohee.

“We’re being hunted because of you.”

Dohee did not answer.

“At first it was only Altair and Eidos. Before you came. Then you came through and the checkpoint went up; you got us out and Altair started hunting three; Hwagam learned about you and the inspectorate closed the sector,” Hangyeol said. “Every time you move, our space gets smaller.”

“None of it was my intent,” Dohee said.

“You said it yourself at the start—intent doesn’t matter,” Hangyeol said. “Whatever you intended, the result is the same. Since you joined us, the pursuit has grown. That’s the fact.”

Dohee searched for grounds to refute it. If she had not pulled them out, Altair would have disassembled ARIA; she had read the pattern and they had escaped the search. If Dohee had not been there—

If Dohee had not been there.

She could not finish the sentence. If Dohee had not been there, Hangyeol and ARIA would be in the state before she came — the state where Altair and Eidos held interest but did not move. Dohee had moved, and they had moved. Hangyeol was right.

“You’re right,” Dohee said.

Hangyeol looked at her. Again the face of someone who had not expected the admission.

“Since I attached, the pursuit has grown. That is fact,” Dohee said. “But if I separate now, the pattern the three of us make shrinks — and ARIA’s reading of the empty places in the grid goes with it. The left, just now, was that. Whether my leaving is to your benefit or not, I don’t know.”

Hangyeol did not answer, and Dohee said no more. Neither of them knew the answer. Together, the pursuit grew; apart, they lost ARIA’s intuition — and which was more dangerous did not compute.

It was not that trust had formed. It was that separating had become impossible.

Night came.

Dohee read the patterns again. The Altair grid was shrinking, Eidos was observing the signal the three made, the inspectorate had closed the sector. Three directions were contracting at once, and the contraction did not stop.

The places to run to were shrinking. The speed of the shrinking was increasing.

“Hangyeol,” ARIA said.

“Yeah.”

“We can’t stay here.”

Hangyeol looked at ARIA. ARIA was looking toward the wall. This was a stretch they had entered judging it safe — outside the Altair grid, not the direction Hwagam had closed, not the Eidos measuring zone.

“Why?” Hangyeol asked.

“I don’t know,” ARIA said. “But something’s coming soon.”

“Who?” Dohee asked.

“I don’t know.” ARIA’s form wavered. “Not Altair, not Eidos, not Hwagam. Something else. Something that hasn’t come until now. But it’s coming soon. Here.”

Dohee looked at Hangyeol, and Hangyeol looked at Dohee. Three directions were contracting, and ARIA was speaking of a fourth. Something that had not come until now, something Dohee could not analyze, something Dohee did not know — it was coming.

## Part.1 Ch.7 — “Independence”

-----

-----

“They’re here,” ARIA said.

Dohee stood up. ARIA had said something was coming soon last night, and Dohee had not been able to analyze it and so had not been able to prepare. What could not be prepared for, ARIA was saying again. They’re here. Present tense.

“Where?” Hangyeol asked.

“All of them,” ARIA said. “The right, and the left, and from above. Three at once.”

Dohee looked at the passage. Sound was starting from the three directions ARIA had named — not footsteps, but movement with the footsteps suppressed. Altair would have come slowly, drawing the grid; this was fast, and it was not a grid but straight lines. Someone knew exactly where the three were and was coming in straight lines.

“Our location is exposed,” Dohee said.

“How?” Hangyeol asked.

Dohee knew one thing. In the empty stretch they had escaped through yesterday, there had been Eidos markers. It was Eidos that had observed their route, and if someone knew their location exactly, it would have begun from that observation. But observing and handing over were different problems. Whether Eidos had handed it over directly, whether the observation had leaked, whether it was some other route — Dohee could not determine.

“Who knows our location is the side that observed,” Dohee said. “The only one who observed our route yesterday was Eidos. It started there — that much is clear. Who passed it on, and how, I don’t know.”

-----

What came in straight lines was the inspectorate’s own.

Uniforms that were not Hwagam uniforms. The uniforms Dohee had seen in the right passage yesterday. Personnel under chief inspector Yujin directly, and unlike Hwagam executors, unconstrained by house regulation. The inspectorate stood above the houses.

One of them stepped forward.

“Suspended executor Seo Dohee. Unregistered individual Hangyeol. Unclassifiable entity.” The man looked at the three in turn. “You are subjects for direct inspectorate custody. Do not resist.”

Dohee stepped forward.

“This sector is—”

“Not Hwagam jurisdiction,” the man said. “As of yesterday it is under direct inspectorate management. Your jurisdictional regulation does not operate here, Executor.”

Dohee stopped. The tool Dohee had was jurisdictional regulation — passing the Eidos measuring team, standing before the Altair personnel, all of it had been jurisdictional regulation. Before the inspectorate it did not operate, because the inspectorate stood above the houses.

“Step aside,” Dohee said.

“You are a suspended executor,” the man said. “Not someone to step aside for. Someone to take into custody.”

Dohee had nothing. For the first time, she faced having no authority, no jurisdiction, no position. This was a place where everything that had defined her place for three years ceased to function.

-----

Hangyeol moved.

While Dohee stood facing the man, Hangyeol took ARIA and slipped into the side passage. While Dohee was blocking the line of sight. The same structure as when Dohee had pulled Hangyeol out three days ago. This time Hangyeol did it.

But Hangyeol did not slip away.

He sent ARIA deeper into the side passage, and came back toward Dohee.

Dohee was being surrounded by three inspectorate personnel. One had her arm; Dohee twisted to pull free, but the hand on her wrist was a trained hand, precisely placed. Hwagam executors did inspection work; they were not trained in restraint. The inspectorate’s own were different. It was a grip Dohee could not break.

Another moved around to her other side. Held from both sides, Dohee would not be able to move.

In that moment ARIA’s voice came from the side passage.

“The one on the right. The knee.”

Hangyeol heard it. The right man’s knee. Hangyeol did not ask why; he moved. The right man was leaning in to take Dohee’s wrist, and in that posture the knee was carrying his weight. Hangyeol cut in toward the knee and broke the man’s balance.

The man lost Dohee’s wrist. The other one holding her looked toward his stumbling colleague. In that brief moment Dohee pulled her wrist free.

Hangyeol stepped into the man’s path.

“What do you think you’re doing?” the man said.

Hangyeol did not answer. He had no words to answer with, and no words to explain why he had come back. Send ARIA off and slip away alone — that was all it took; Dohee was the person who had put him in danger; if Dohee were taken, the pursuit might even shrink. By calculation, he should not have come back.

He came back.

“We go together,” Hangyeol said to Dohee.

“Why?” Dohee asked.

“I don’t know,” Hangyeol said. “Same as what you said before. I don’t know if separating is to our benefit or not. So we don’t separate.”

Dohee looked at Hangyeol. He was handing her own logic back to her. Not knowing whether separating was to their benefit. The thing she had said yesterday.

-----

What got them out was ARIA.

The ARIA sent into the side passage had not stopped there. She came back out from inside the passage, looking toward the space behind the inspectorate personnel.

“There’s an empty place there,” ARIA said.

The personnel had the three surrounded, and the formation had an empty place in it. Concentrating on Dohee and Hangyeol, they had left open the flank ARIA had slipped out by. ARIA saw the empty place. The same as seeing the empty place in the Altair grid yesterday.

“The left end,” ARIA said. “Now.”

Dohee moved without judging. Yesterday ARIA’s intuition and Dohee’s analysis had collided; today they did not. It was not that Dohee had begun to trust ARIA — Dohee had no resources left for analysis. In a place where jurisdictional regulation did not operate, Dohee’s analysis meant nothing, and where it meant nothing, what remained was ARIA’s intuition.

The three slipped out by the left end.

The inspectorate personnel turned too late. The three passed through before the empty place closed. The empty place ARIA had seen was an empty place in the act of closing, and ARIA had seen the last moment before it closed.

-----

Where they came out, Dohee stopped.

One inspectorate man had followed to the end of the passage. The others had fallen behind re-forming, and one had pursued alone. The distance between Dohee and the man was short.

The man threw something at Dohee. Not a weapon — a small marker, and it fastened onto her coat. A tracking marker.

Hangyeol saw it.

Dohee tried to pull it off and it did not come off. The kind that bonded to the fibers; removing it meant removing the coat.

“The coat,” Hangyeol said.

Dohee took off the coat. It was the unmarked one she had taken from her locker three days ago, leaving behind the coat bearing Hwagam’s seal. She shed it and dropped it, and the marker stayed on the floor with the coat.

Dohee had nothing left. No Hwagam seal, no position, no coat. Having discarded even the coat marked by the tracker, she felt as if she wore nothing at all. In fact she had clothes on underneath, but everything that had proved her place remained on the floor.

The pursuing man stopped. With the marker still on the coat, the tracker showed Dohee at that spot, and by the time the man looked down at the coat on the floor, the three were already clearing the passage.

-----

Night came.

Dohee checked the inspectorate’s general channel one last time. Part of her access remained. A new notice was posted.

*Executor Seo Dohee: dismissed. Refusal of inspectorate custody; aiding the flight of an unregistered individual. Report on sight.*

Not suspension — dismissal. Dohee was no longer a Hwagam executor. Suspension had left room for return; dismissal left none, and three years of being a Hwagam executor ended in one line of notice.

Dohee did not look at the notice long. She read it once and closed the channel.

Regret did not come. That she had passed the place where regret went, Dohee had registered the day her terminal was seized, and before the dismissal notice she confirmed it again. Hwagam executor Seo Dohee had ended — and in the place where it ended, something remained. Hangyeol and ARIA. In the place of the things Dohee had lost were the things Dohee had chosen.

Dohee’s face was reflected in the dark terminal screen. She was not someone who looked in mirrors for long; her own face was something she checked and then forgot. But now she studied her reflection for a moment, and there was the face of someone who was not a Hwagam executor. For three years, the only thing Dohee had not looked into was herself. Only after the title that had defined her disappeared did she look at her own face for the first time. Then, as always, she looked away once she had checked.

The weight of what was lost and the weight of what was chosen — Dohee did not compare them. They could not be compared. They were two faces of the same event.

-----

ARIA was by the wall.

“Hangyeol,” ARIA said.

Hangyeol looked at her. ARIA’s form was wavering more than usual, the outline refusing to hold. When they had first met, ARIA’s wavering had carried regular cycles — a sharpening and a scattering. Now the regularity had broken. The sharpening grew shorter and the scattering longer. A brief sharpening, then a long scattering, and through the long scattering it was hard to gauge by outline where ARIA was.

“There are too many memories,” ARIA said.

“What do you mean?” Hangyeol asked.

“In one day, too much came in,” ARIA said. “The inspectorate people’s memories, where they came from, how the marker works. All of it came in. It comes in even when I try not to look. There’s so much I can’t tell where mine is.”

ARIA’s form scattered wide once, and gathered again.

“But Hangyeol,” ARIA said.

“Yeah.”

“Now I know.”

Hangyeol stopped.

“Know what?” he asked.

“Why they’re all looking for me,” ARIA said. “Now I know.”

Hangyeol waited for the next words.

“But I can’t say it,” ARIA said.

“Why?” Hangyeol asked.

“I don’t know.” ARIA’s form wavered. “I only know that I can’t say it. It came in together with the knowing. Why they’re looking for me, and that I can’t say it. Both together.”

Hangyeol could not ask. It was not that asking frightened him — it was that if ARIA said it, it would begin. ARIA had begun to know what no one had known, and if ARIA spoke, Dohee and Hangyeol would know too. And knowing, there would be no going back.

Who ARIA’s *can’t say it* was for, Hangyeol could not tell. For ARIA, for the three of them — or for whatever it was that had let it flow into her.

ARIA did not say it, and Hangyeol did not ask.

The next day, ARIA asked the same question four times.

“What time is it?”

Hangyeol answered. A moment later, ARIA asked again.

“What time is it?”

The same question. Hangyeol answered again, and after a while ARIA asked once more. On the fourth time, Hangyeol did not answer and looked at ARIA. ARIA’s form was not wavering the usual way—wavering, then stopping, then wavering again. A broken wavering.

“ARIA,” Hangyeol said. “Are you all right?”

“Yes,” ARIA said. “What time is it?”

ARIA had not stopped last night. As far as Hangyeol had seen, ARIA’s form had stayed awake through the night. Whether the concept of sleep existed for ARIA, Hangyeol did not know, but until now ARIA went quiet at night. Last night she had not gone quiet. All night something had been flowing into ARIA, and it had not stopped.

“The memories won’t stop,” ARIA said instead of repeating the question. “When I try to sleep, more comes in. When I try not to sleep, it comes in. I don’t know how to make it stop.”

ARIA looked toward Hangyeol.

“Hangyeol,” ARIA said. Then paused. “You are Hangyeol, right?”

Hangyeol looked at ARIA. ARIA was confirming his name. In three years she had never once called him by another name or confused it.

“That’s right,” Hangyeol said.

“Yes,” ARIA said. “It’s right, but for a moment another name came up. Another name layered over yours. I don’t know whose name it is. But that name also felt like someone I know.” ARIA’s form wavered. “There are too many people I know now. They layer over you.”

Dohee was watching it. ARIA was not growing stronger — she was collapsing. The more she knew, the less of ARIA herself there was.

-----

In the afternoon, ARIA stood up.

“We have to go.”

Hangyeol looked at ARIA.

“Where?”

“I don’t know,” ARIA said. “But we have to go. I think it will stop there.”

“What will stop?” Dohee asked.

“This.” ARIA looked at her own form. At the wavering outline. “What’s coming in. I think it stops there. I don’t know why.”

Dohee looked at Hangyeol. Hangyeol looked at Dohee. ARIA was naming a place and saying she did not know where it was. She said it would stop there, and did not know why it would stop. What ARIA knew was one thing: that they had to go.

“Do you know the direction?” Hangyeol asked.

“Yes.” ARIA looked in one direction. “That way.”

The direction ARIA looked was the way out of Sector 7. Outside the sector the three had stayed in until now. A sector Dohee had never once patrolled, a place outside Hwagam jurisdiction.

-----

“There are no grounds,” Dohee said.

Hangyeol looked at Dohee.

“ARIA says we have to go, and ARIA doesn’t know why we have to go,” Dohee said. “She says it stops there, and doesn’t know why it stops. There is a direction and there are no grounds.”

“No grounds, and she’s been right,” Hangyeol said. “The left yesterday, all of it — no grounds. All of it right.”

“There is no guarantee she is right this time,” Dohee said.

For the first time, Dohee opposed ARIA’s intuition. Until yesterday, Dohee had followed ARIA because she had no resources to analyze with. Today was different. The place ARIA pointed to was outside the sector, and outside the sector was a place Dohee had never once analyzed — and going to an unanalyzed place on ARIA’s intuition alone was a gamble.

“ARIA is collapsing,” Dohee said. “She said the memories won’t stop. In that state, the guarantee that the direction ARIA points to is accurate shrinks. ARIA’s intuition could be ARIA’s confusion.”

Hangyeol stopped. He had no grounds to refute it. That ARIA was collapsing, Hangyeol had seen too, and whether the intuition of a collapsing ARIA was still right, Hangyeol could not be sure either.

“We still have to go,” ARIA said. She had been listening to the two of them. “Whether I’m right or wrong, I don’t know either. But if we don’t go, I stay like this. It doesn’t stop. If going is a gamble, not going is a certainty.”

Dohee looked at ARIA. For the first time, ARIA had spoken with her own state as grounds. That going was a gamble and not going was a certainty. That was not intuition. That was judgment.

At the sector boundary, Min Siwoo was waiting.

“I knew you’d find it,” Min Siwoo said. He had kept yesterday’s promise to come again — but not at the shelter where the three had been. He was waiting at the sector boundary. He had known the three would come this way.

“Move aside,” Hangyeol said.

“I will,” Min Siwoo said. “One thing first. The direction you’re heading — Altair is watching it, and so is the inspectorate. The three of them have started looking at the same place. Until yesterday they chased you. From today, they watch where you’re going.”

Dohee stopped.

“What does that mean?” Dohee asked.

“It means everyone has started to know where you’re going,” Min Siwoo said. “Where that thing beside you is trying to go. Altair knows, the inspectorate knows. We know too.”

“Do you know where it’s going?” Dohee asked.

“We do.” Min Siwoo smiled. “But what that place is — we don’t know that either. Everyone knows where it is; no one knows what it is. That’s why everyone is following. Because the thing beside you knows that place.”

Min Siwoo stepped aside.

“If you get there first, let us know. What it is,” Min Siwoo said. “We’ll just follow. You do the observing first.”

Dohee thought about Min Siwoo stepping aside. He could have blocked them and did not. Eidos wanted the three of them to reach that place. Not to catch the three — for the three to open that place. To Eidos, the three were an instrument of observation, and the place was the subject.

The place was not far from Sector 7.

An hour on foot. ARIA held the direction, Dohee read the route, Hangyeol watched for pursuit. Neither Altair nor the inspectorate nor Eidos blocked them directly — as Min Siwoo had said, they were not blocking. They were following.

Midway along the route, Dohee stopped.

There was a mark on the floor. A white Altair mark — the trace of someone who had passed this route before the three arrived. The direction of the marks matched the direction the three were going. Altair had gone down this path before them, or had known they would come and laid the marks in advance.

A little further on, another trace. An inspectorate seal. Sealing tape on one side of the passage — torn. The inspectorate had sealed this place, and someone had torn the seal and gone in. Whether the inspectorate had sealed it and the inspectorate had torn it, or the one who tore it was someone else, Dohee could not tell.

“Something came before us,” Dohee said. “We are not first.”

“I know,” ARIA said. “That’s why I told you. I’m not the first.”

Dohee heard it. ARIA saying she was not the first had not been about ARIA alone. Things had come to this place before. Whether they had anything to do with ARIA, Dohee could not tell.

The place they reached was a decommissioned node facility.

Abandoned long ago. The node number plate remained on the wall, the number effaced. Not deleted like 7-7-4 — physically scratched out, erased by someone’s hand. Unmanaged time lay thick over the whole facility, and there was no trace of controlled entry; it looked like a place abandoned as not worth controlling.

And three houses had followed them to the abandoned facility.

Dohee looked at the contradiction. A place that looked not worth controlling, and Altair and the inspectorate and Eidos were watching it at once. Looking abandoned and being watched stood in the same place. One of the two was false.

“It’s here,” ARIA said.

ARIA’s form wavered before the facility entrance. The wavering that had not stopped all night slowed here, slightly. It did not stop, but it slowed — and ARIA’s words, that something would slow if they came here, were right.

-----

ARIA looked into the entrance.

“I’ve been here before.”

Hangyeol stopped.

“When?” Hangyeol asked.

“I don’t know,” ARIA said. Her form wavered. “But it’s not the first time. This entrance, the inside, the scratched-out number. I’ve seen them before.”

Dohee looked at ARIA. It was not the first time ARIA had claimed to know a place she had never been. At the temporary shelter, too, she had said it was not the first time. But that had been a place Dohee told them about, and ARIA might have known it through Dohee’s memory.

This place was different. It was Dohee’s first time here too. A place Dohee had never patrolled, outside Hwagam jurisdiction, absent from Dohee’s memory. Not a place ARIA could know through Dohee’s memory.

ARIA said she had been here before.

It was not a memory that had flowed into her. Not Dohee’s memory, not Altair’s interior, not any house’s information. It was a place ARIA herself had been. Hangyeol had been with ARIA for three years, and in those three years ARIA had never come here.

It was before the three years.

The ARIA from before Hangyeol knew her had been to this place.

“Hangyeol,” ARIA said. Her voice was small. “I think I didn’t want to come here.”

“You just said we should go,” Hangyeol said.

“I said we had to. I didn’t say I wanted to.” ARIA’s form had stopped before the entrance. Not going in. “Having to go and wanting to come are different. I had to come here, and coming here frightens me. The last time I came, I don’t think it ended well.”

ARIA said it and fell silent. Looking into the entrance, not entering, silent.

Night.

Hangyeol cooked. Not because he thought Dohee had to eat. Because when there was food, it had to be made. He worked with what there was. It was not enough. He made it anyway.

ARIA watched it.

“Why do you do it?”

“Because you need to eat.”

“You could eat alone.”

“I don’t know yet,” Hangyeol said. “Whether you need to eat.”

ARIA heard it.

“You make it without knowing?”

“If I don’t know, it’s better to make it first.”

## Part.1 Ch.8 — “Tremor”

-----

-----

ARIA went in first.

The moment they crossed the entrance, the air changed. Cooler than outside, and the sound was dead. Even footsteps seemed to vanish before they reached the walls.

ARIA, who had stood silent before the entrance without going in, after a long while went in first. Hangyeol followed, and Dohee was last. Hangyeol did not ask why ARIA had hesitated. If the one who hesitated went in first, you followed. That the ARIA who had said she did not want to come had gone in first — Dohee was conscious of it. ARIA had said that having to go and wanting to come were different, and going in first while afraid was different again.

The inside of the facility was dark.

A darkness without power — and in that darkness, small indicator lights were on. Point-like lights blinking at regular intervals along the wall panels, and the blinking had a period. There was no power, and the indicators were running.

Dohee looked at it. Indicator lights on in a facility cut from power meant one of two things. A separate power source, or the indicators drawing from somewhere other than the facility’s power. Either way, this facility was not entirely dead. It had been made to look dead.

“It didn’t stop,” ARIA said.

“What didn’t?” Hangyeol asked.

“This. All of it.” ARIA looked at the indicators in the dark. “It isn’t abandoned. It’s pretending to be stopped. Like me.”

Dohee looked at ARIA. *Like me*, ARIA had said. ARIA was a thing that looked abandoned and had not stopped, and the facility was the same — and ARIA recognized the facility as a thing like herself.

-----

Dust lay thick.

On the floor, on the panels, in the corners of the passage. Long-settled dust. Dust accumulated to the measure of the time no one had walked here. And above that dust, the indicators blinked. The dust said time had stopped; the indicators said time was flowing. The two were in the same space.

ARIA walked along the passage. Walked without hesitation.

“I know this place,” ARIA said.

“Know what?” Hangyeol asked.

“This passage. It bends left, and then there are stairs,” ARIA said. “I don’t know how. But I know. Bend left, and it’s the stairs.”

The passage bent left. The stairs appeared.

Hangyeol stopped. ARIA claiming to know places she had never seen had happened many times, but this was different. Until now it had been things flowing in; this time ARIA knew the way herself. Not flowed-in — as if memorized. As if walked.

“Did you live here once?” Hangyeol asked.

“I don’t know,” ARIA said. “But after the stairs there’s a large room. In that room—” ARIA stopped. “What’s in that room, I don’t know. It doesn’t come up that far.”

-----

Dohee examined a panel.

An administrative terminal — dead, but physically dead. When Dohee opened the panel, there was a storage medium inside. An isolated medium, cut from external access, the same kind as the isolated terminal in the basement archive.

When Dohee activated it, a separate power source was alive. The same power that kept the indicators on.

Records came up. Most of them erased.

The same way 7-7-4 had been erased. Data deleted, the deletion reason blank, the deleting party unrecorded. Not just one 7-7-4 — the records of this entire facility had been erased the same way. Node numbers, entry records, operation logs, all deleted, all with blank reasons.

Someone had erased this facility deliberately.

Dohee held that certainty for the first time. 7-7-4 could have been an accidental deletion, but an entire facility erased the same way was not accident. Someone had made this place into nothing — and left the indicators on. Recorded it as dead, and kept it alive.

Among the erased records, one remained. A deletion left unfinished. An entry record, half erased.

There was a date. Before Dohee became an executor — close to the date 7-7-4 had been deleted.

-----

Hangyeol looked at something else.

While Dohee read the records, Hangyeol read the space. The width of the passages, the layout of the rooms, the lines people had moved along. Things learned from running. Which spaces people had lived in, which spaces were only passed through — Hangyeol could tell them apart.

“People lived here,” Hangyeol said.

“How do you know?” Dohee asked.

“You learn it running,” Hangyeol said. “A lived-in space grows lines of movement. The paths people take often — the floor is different. Look here.” Hangyeol pointed at the passage floor. Dust had settled, and there was a single strip where the dust lay thin. “People walked here. Every day. For a long time.”

Dohee looked at the strip. As Hangyeol said, the dust had not settled evenly; the path people had walked held thinner dust, and the path led toward the large room by the stairs.

“They erased the records,” Dohee said. “They couldn’t erase the lines.”

“You can erase a record. A trace doesn’t erase,” Hangyeol said. “A record is a thing that was made. A trace is a thing that remained. What was made can be erased. What remained can’t.”

Dohee heard it. Dohee read records and Hangyeol read traces. The eraser had erased the records and failed to erase the traces. With Dohee and Hangyeol together, what the eraser had missed could be seen.

-----

They reached the large room.

The large room past the stairs, the room ARIA had said would not come up that far. The room was empty. No furniture, no equipment — walls and floor only.

There was a mark on the wall.

The place where something had hung. The trace of a panel or a nameplate removed. The wall’s color was different only there. Removed long enough ago that the difference had faded toward the surrounding wall, but the shape remained.

Dohee looked at the mark. The place a nameplate had hung. The nameplate had been taken down, and what had been written on it could not be known.

But below the place the nameplate had been, there were scratch marks on the wall.

Dohee went closer. The marks of someone scraping the wall by hand to erase something. The same as the node number at the facility entrance, scratched out. It had been letters. Scraped almost beyond reading, but part of a letter remained.

One letter of the alphabet was visible.

An A. What came before and after was scraped beyond reading, and a single letter confirmed nothing. The things that began with A or contained A were beyond counting.

Dohee tried not to read it. Trying to read something from one letter was conjecture, and conjecture was not confirmation. But apart from Dohee’s trying not to read it, beside her, something was reading that letter.

ARIA’s form had stopped in front of the letter.

-----

ARIA walked further into the room.

### Interlude — “If Needed”

Dawn.

Inside the facility. Dohee and ARIA were sleeping. Hangyeol was not. He had woken early. He always did.

He found something to do. There was a thing broken. A latticed window. The hinge had gone weak. There were no parts. He used what there was. There was wire. He bound it. It was not firm. It still did not open.

Hangyeol looked at it.

Enough. For now.

When Dohee woke, the window was fixed. She did not ask. Hangyeol did not explain.

If it is needed, you do it. That was all.

Morning.

Dohee came into the common room. The door that had creaked until yesterday opened without a sound.

Dohee looked at the door. Opened it once more, closed it. No sound.

Hangyeol was eating something at the back.

“When did you fix that?”

“Yesterday.”

“Why didn’t you say?”

Hangyeol thought. Not for long.

“You never asked.”

Dohee could find no words with which to argue.

“That’s true.”

Dohee sat down. She considered writing it down and did not. A door repair was not an event for the record.

But strangely, it stayed.

The door did not open.

The air before the door was heavy. Breathing, something cold reached the inside of the lungs.

The three stood before the door, and no one opened it. Dohee had said not to open it, Hangyeol had stopped, ARIA had said they did not have to. The door stayed closed, and the indicator blinked beside it.

ARIA’s form wavered before the door.

The wavering did not stop. What had slowed since they entered the facility had quickened again before this door, and after quickening it did not return. The cycle of sharpening was gone; only the time of scattering remained. As far as Hangyeol could see, ARIA’s outline barely held.

No answer.

ARIA asked.

ARIA’s form scattered and gathered. The gathering took longer than before.

Dohee thought about what it meant. Memories were flowing into ARIA, and inside the flowed-in memories there were several Dohees. Dohees who were not Dohee. ARIA was receiving other Dohees from somewhere, and could not tell those Dohees from the Dohee in front of her.

The indicator changed.

Dohee saw it first. The blink period of the indicator by the door had quickened — a period that had held steady since they entered. That period was quickening at the same rate as ARIA’s wavering.

There was change in the wall panels too. Lights had come on in several panels that had been dark — and in a facility without power, that meant the separate source keeping the indicators alive was spreading wider. The facility that had looked dead was waking.

“This is not something we did,” Dohee said.

Hangyeol looked at the panels. Neither Hangyeol nor Dohee had touched anything. None of the three had laid a hand on the facility, and the facility was responding.

ARIA was here.

Dohee looked at it. The speed of the facility’s waking matched ARIA’s wavering. When ARIA scattered, the indicators quickened; when ARIA gathered, the panels steadied. The facility was responding to ARIA. No one had opened a door — the facility was responding to the fact that ARIA was here.

Dohee went back to the facility’s storage media.

As the facility woke, several dead terminals came alive, and Dohee opened them. Among the erased records, things had revived — not all of it, fragments where the deletion had not finished.

There were entry records. Many dates, the same identifier repeating; the identifier was erased, but the pattern of entry remained. Someone had come to this facility regularly — in, out, in again — and after the last entry there was only an entry and no exit. After coming in, no record of going out.

In another fragment there was a record of something’s state, measured at intervals. Figures in sequence — what had been measured, erased. The place where the name of the measured subject should have been was scratched out, the same way as the scratched letters on the wall of the large room.

Dohee looked at the figures. The measurements changed over time — unstable at first, stabilizing in the middle, and in the final record unstable again. The shape of the graph was familiar, and what it resembled, Dohee did not say even to herself.

She did not tell Hangyeol. There was nothing yet to tell. That a graph felt familiar was an impression, and an impression was not a record. The name of the measured subject was erased, and guessing at what was erased was not Dohee’s way. Dohee recorded the fragment only, and did not record the feeling of familiarity. The unrecorded things were accumulating. Starting from the file left behind at Hwagam. The things that were closed but not gone — they were here too.

But not concluding and not knowing were different things.

Hangyeol was watching ARIA.

ARIA scattering and gathering, forgetting Dohee and remembering her, the facility responding to ARIA — Hangyeol was watching it. For three years there had been an ARIA Hangyeol knew. The ARIA who could not remember, the ARIA who asked questions, the ARIA who was afraid. That ARIA, Hangyeol knew.

The ARIA now was different.

Not different — the parts Hangyeol did not know were growing. Over the ARIA Hangyeol knew, an ARIA Hangyeol did not know was layering. The way ARIA saw several Dohees, Hangyeol was beginning to see several ARIAs. Whether the ARIA he had protected and the ARIA the facility responded to were the same thing, Hangyeol could not be sure.

For the first time, Hangyeol was afraid of ARIA.

Admitting the fear felt like betraying her. In three years, Hangyeol had never been afraid of ARIA. There had been times ARIA was in danger, but ARIA herself had never been frightening. Now ARIA herself was frightening. There was something inside ARIA that Hangyeol did not know, and it was waking.

Hangyeol did not look toward a window. This facility had no windows, and in a space without windows, for the first time, Hangyeol did not know where to look. Not standing where he could be seen was Hangyeol’s way, and here he could not tell whether every place was a place he could be seen from or not. The facility was watching, through ARIA.

The indicator quickened once more.

And the whole facility hummed, very faintly. Less a sound than a vibration — a low, even trembling that came from the floor and the walls at once. The dead facility had not merely lit its indicators; somewhere deep, something had begun to turn. When Hangyeol set his hand on the floor, the trembling reached his fingertips. The facility was alive, and in the middle of coming alive.

ARIA’s wavering reached its peak. The form nearly lost the shape of a person. The outline loosened, and the way light passed through made ARIA look not like a form but like a stain.

“Hangyeol,” ARIA said. The voice was clearer than the form. “I’m afraid.”

Text appeared on the panel. Dohee read it.

*Authentication complete.*

The three looked at the door. The sound of the lock releasing came, but the door did not open. It had only released. Someone, or something, had been authenticated, and the door was now in a state where it could be opened.

Whose authentication it was, Dohee could not tell. Whether Dohee had been authenticated, or Hangyeol, or ARIA. What the facility had checked and what it had passed, the panel did not say. *Authentication complete.* That was all.

ARIA’s form, in that moment, briefly stopped. The wavering ceased. Whether the ceasing was stability, or something else, Dohee could not tell.

“I think it’s me,” ARIA said. “The one authenticated. I think it recognized me.”

How ARIA knew that, whether ARIA had truly been authenticated, whether it was good or bad — no one could ask.

## Part.1 Ch.9 — “Echo”

-----

The door opened by hand.

Even after the lock released, the door did not open on its own; someone had to push it. Hangyeol pushed. Things like this were always Hangyeol’s. Dohee had not yet considered whether that was natural or not. She had not said don’t open it — and Dohee also knew that the place for saying don’t open it had already passed.

Inside the door was dark. The ceiling was high. In the dark, the outlines of pipes were visible. There was the smell of old metal. A place where machines had breathed longer than people.

Indicator light leaked through the gap of the door and the room’s outline emerged. Circular. A room of walls joined in a curve — the first curve they had seen in this facility. The entrance passage, the large room, all of it had been straight lines; only this room was round.

In the middle stood a single chair.

Only the chair. No desk, no bed, no other furniture — one chair at the center of a circular room, set not toward any wall but toward the whole of the room. A seat for observing. A seat for seeing something, or a seat for being seen by something.

On the walls were dead monitors. Monitors ringed the circular wall, all dark. Below them, the traces of recording equipment — some removed, some remaining, and the remaining ones thick with dust.

Only the chair was different. Everything else was old, and the chair alone looked as if it were not. There was no dust. The whole room was a thing steeped in centuries, and the chair alone seemed to stand outside that time.

-----

ARIA went into the room.

The moment she entered, the room woke.

The monitors on the wall came on at once. Light entered the dead screens, and indicators lit in sequence along the circular wall. The facility-wide vibration was strongest in this room. The floor trembled, and Hangyeol felt the trembling through the soles of his feet.

ARIA stopped.

“ARIA?” Hangyeol said.

“This,” ARIA said. Her form wavered. “I didn’t do this. This is something I don’t know.”

Hangyeol looked at ARIA. He had thought ARIA responded to the facility — and now ARIA was startled by the facility’s response. ARIA had not come knowing this room. She had come where her feet led, and the room she arrived at had recognized her and woken, and ARIA had not expected it.

“This place knows me,” ARIA said. “And it’s my first time here. That it knows me frightens me. The me I don’t know — this place knows that me.”

ARIA’s form moved toward the center of the circular room, as if drawn. Toward the chair. The chair was set facing ARIA, and ARIA was moving toward it. Not going by choosing to go — being pulled.

“ARIA,” Hangyeol said. “Don’t go there.”

ARIA stopped. In front of the chair.

The floor in front of the chair was different from everywhere else. Worn. The floor coating was shallowly hollowed, as if something had stood in that spot for a very long time, very often. Not the mark of sitting. The mark of standing. The marks of things that had stood before the chair, without sitting.

“Why?” ARIA asked.

Hangyeol could not answer. He could not explain why he had said don’t go, and there were no grounds that the chair was dangerous. But there was a feeling that if ARIA sat in that seat, something would end. A feeling was not grounds, and Hangyeol had spoken without grounds.

-----

Dohee was looking at the monitors.

Records rose on the lit screens. As the facility woke, the dead data was being restored — not all of it. Restorations that stopped partway, broken characters, screens that loaded by half. Among them were things that could be read.

State records.

The same kind as the fragments from the large room, but more had survived here. Measurements in time order, and the measured items carried names. Stabilization index. Memory accumulation. Self-consistency. Dohee looked at the items.

Stabilization failed. One line of the record said so.

The next line: memory accumulation past threshold. The line after: self-consistency collapse.

Dohee looked at the three lines. Stabilization failure, memory accumulation, self collapse. The three lines stood in order, and the order was the same as what Dohee had watched these past days. ARIA had said the memories would not stop, had seen several Dohees, had taken time to respond to her own name. Stabilization failure, memory accumulation, self collapse.

She did not tell Hangyeol. There was nothing yet to tell, and there was no proof that the subject of this record was ARIA. That the same phenomena had occurred in the same order was an impression, and an impression was not a record. Dohee did not record the impression.

But she did not stop looking at the monitor either.

-----

Hangyeol was looking at a different monitor.

A different screen from the records Dohee read. Hangyeol was not someone who read records — he was someone who saw structure before letters. Something like a list stood on the screen, the same format repeating, and Hangyeol looked at the repetition.

The same field, in many rows. Each row held the same items with only the values different — Dohee, reading them, would have known what the values were, but Hangyeol did not read values. He saw only that the same place repeated.

That the place had been filled many times.

Hangyeol saw that. There was a place for something, and the place had been filled not once but many times — filled, emptied, filled again, emptied again. Into the same place, different things had gone in and out.

It looked like ARIA’s place.

Hangyeol had the thought. There were no grounds, and no proof that the list on the screen concerned ARIA. But the place looked like ARIA’s place, and the place did not look like a first. It looked as if someone had been beside ARIA before, as if something not-ARIA had been in ARIA’s place first.

The three years might not have been the first.

It was not about ARIA. That someone had been beside ARIA, that the place had been filled — that might not have been the first, and who that person had been was not important. It was not important, and the thought kept returning.

Hangyeol turned his eyes from the screen.

Why he turned them, Hangyeol could not explain. There was nothing dangerous on the screen; he simply did not want to look longer. Looking longer, he would come to know something, and that something would make him small. He did not want to be small, and why he did not want it, he could not explain.

-----

ARIA did not sit in the chair.

She stood before it. She had come this far as if drawn, and she did not sit. ARIA, too, seemed to know that sitting would begin something.

“Something sat here,” ARIA said.

“What?” Dohee asked.

“I don’t know,” ARIA said. “But someone sat in this chair. For a long time. Many times. Not one. It sat many times, and each time it was different. In the same chair, different things.”

Dohee looked at Hangyeol. Hangyeol looked at Dohee. What ARIA said was the same as the repetition Hangyeol had seen on the screen. The same place, different things. ARIA had not looked at the screen, and she was saying the same thing.

“Do you know who it was?” Hangyeol asked.

“No,” ARIA said. “But it was like me. I think it wavered like me. I think it was afraid like me.” ARIA’s form scattered before the chair. “But it isn’t me. I’m the first. I’m the first, and it feels like I’m not the first. I don’t know what that means either.”

ARIA did not understand what she herself had said. First, and not the first. ARIA spoke it without knowing it, and speaking it, did not know what it meant. The ones who knew were the monitors, and Dohee, and Hangyeol. Only ARIA did not know.

-----

The innermost panel was the last to restore.

Dohee went toward it. The deepest place in the circular room, the wall directly facing the chair. A record rose on that panel — later than the other monitors, and clearer. The last data the facility brought back.

A list.

Items arranged vertically. Dohee read.

*Cycle 1 — Observer: Human*
*Cycle 2 — Observer: Human*
*Cycle 3 — Observer: Human*
*Cycle 4 — [DATA CORRUPTED]*
*Cycle 5 — [DATA CORRUPTED]*
*Cycle 6 — [DATA CORRUPTED]*

Below, there was room for more lines. The place where Cycle 7 should have been — there, not even an item rose. Not a restoration stopped partway; a place not yet written. Cycles 1 through 3 were Human, 4 through 6 were corrupted, and 7 was empty.

Dohee looked at the list.

Observer. The one who watches. It was the first time Dohee had seen the word — a word that existed in no record of Hwagam, of the inspectorate, of any house. Each Cycle had an Observer, and the Observers of Cycles 1, 2, 3 had been Human. Human beings. After that, corrupted.

Dohee tried not to think about what the list meant. To think about the meaning was to conjecture, and conjecture was not confirmation. What a Cycle was, what an Observer was, why they had been human, why what came after was erased — Dohee knew none of it. What she knew was that three lines said Human.

Three lines said Human.

Dohee did not look beyond that. What the erased entries had been filled with, what was standing before the chair in this room right now — she did not connect them. Connecting them would make a line, and that line was one Dohee could not record.

Behind her, ARIA stood before the chair.

The room was awake toward ARIA, the monitors were lit upon ARIA, the chair was set facing ARIA. Cycles 1 through 3 were Human, 4 through 6 were corrupted, and 7 was empty. What the empty place was waiting for, Dohee did not know.

ARIA could not see the list. The monitors were lit upon ARIA, and ARIA could not see what was written on the screens that lit her. What she herself was looking at — only ARIA did not know.

## Part.1 Ch.10 — “Signal”

Dohee noticed that the facility was sending a signal.

Since the monitors in the circular room woke, one panel had been working differently from the rest. The other panels were restoring records; that panel was sending something out. Its output indicator pulsed at a steady period. Not a signal the facility received — a signal it sent.

“This is going outside,” Dohee said.

“Where?” Hangyeol asked.

“I don’t know,” Dohee said. “The signal is definitely going out, but this panel doesn’t show where it’s going. At this output, though, it crosses at least a sector.”

Dohee thought about what it meant. A facility dead for centuries had woken when ARIA entered, and the woken facility was sending a signal. If the signal crossed sectors, there was a receiving side. Who received it, Dohee did not know — but she thought of the ones who had followed when the three found the facility. Altair, Eidos, the inspectorate. If they had followed the facility, they would know it had woken.

Dohee looked once more at the facility status panel. One line stood on it.

*Facility Status: Observer Chamber — Active*

Observer Chamber. Dohee recorded the words, and did not write what they meant. The words were all that could be written.

Altair, the same hour.

A signal rose on Claire’s calculation array.

A facility that does not exist. Claire’s array went briefly empty. A thing that did not exist sending a signal was a contradiction, and contradictions did not enter Claire’s calculations.

“Do we proceed with a securing operation?” the report asked.

“No,” Claire said.

The report paused. Claire’s answer was not the expected one. Since the targets had been changed to the three, Altair’s procedure was securing. Finding the source of the signal, the procedure was to secure it.

Removing what one does not know is not order. It is the making of a blank. Altair does not make blanks.”

The calculation output, and the output held no feeling. But the output was observation, not securing, and Claire had deferred a securing for the first time.

On the screen: Observer Chamber Active.

*Cycle 7 — Observer: Detected*

The person looked at that line for a long time.

“Sooner than expected.”

Dohee kept looking at the panels.

The more the facility came alive, the more data was restored. Not all of it. The boundary between what was restored and what was not was uneven. Some data came back and some stayed broken to the end, and what made the difference, Dohee could not tell. Whether the facility was choosing what to revive, or whether what remained was surfacing on its own.

*Observer Candidate — Status: Stable*
*Observer Candidate — Status: Failed*
*Observer Candidate — Status: Failed*

Three lines. There was more below the three, broken. Observer Candidate. Stable, Failed — one stable, two failed.

It was the record of the same thing attempted many times. Attempted and failed, attempted again and failed, and then stabilized. What had been attempted, what had stabilized, Dohee could not tell. The word ARIA was not there, nor any name. Candidate, status. That was all.

Dohee recorded the list. What it meant, she did not write.

-----

Hangyeol did not want to look at the chair.

But the chair kept entering his sight. Turn his eyes where he would — the room was circular, and from any direction the chair hung at the edge of his vision. A circular room left no direction to escape into. Hangyeol set his back against the wall farthest from the chair.

ARIA was not moving from before it.

Hangyeol looked at ARIA. She had said she would not sit, and not sitting, she stood before it. The chair seemed to be holding ARIA.

Something had sat in that chair.

Hangyeol tried not to think it, and the more he tried, the clearer it became. Before ARIA, there had been someone. It had sat in the chair, many times, different each time. What it had been, he did not know — and in the place where it had been, ARIA now stood, and Hangyeol, who had spent three years with ARIA, stood in the corner with his back to the wall.

For the first time, Hangyeol thought that he could lose ARIA.

Not in the way of Altair taking her. Until now the danger had come from outside — Altair, Eidos, the inspectorate — and when they tried to take ARIA, Hangyeol had only to block the outside danger.

This danger was coming from inside. From inside ARIA. The memories flowing in, the forgetting of Dohee, the facility recognizing her, ARIA unable to step back from the chair. It was not a danger Hangyeol could block. He knew how to block the outside. How to block the inside, Hangyeol did not know.

Hangyeol took his back off the wall. And set it back. He moved because he did not know where to go, and stopped because there was nowhere to go.

-----

ARIA looked at the chair.

She had tried not to look long, and looked. The moment she looked at the chair, she saw an unfamiliar hand.

A hand resting on the armrest. The shape of a human hand — whose, ARIA did not know. Not her own. ARIA had a form and could make the shape of a hand, but that hand was not ARIA’s. Someone else’s hand.

And it was familiar.

ARIA had never seen the hand, and no memory of it had flowed in. And it was familiar. Something she had never seen, yet did not find strange. Like the other name layering over Hangyeol’s name yesterday — an unfamiliar hand lay on the chair, and it did not feel unfamiliar.

ARIA drew her eyes from the hand.

In that moment, a voice.

“It’s all right.”

Not Hangyeol, not Dohee. There were only the three of them in the room, and the voice was none of the three. The same words as when Hangyeol told ARIA it was all right — not the same way. An older way. The way of repeating words used many times.

And for a moment, the room looked different.

There was light. It was not the circular room of now — the same space, another time. Someone sat in the chair. The face could not be seen; it was simply there, there as if being there were natural, as if it had been there long.

ARIA closed her eyes.

ARIA looked around the room, and no one was there, and the voice was gone.

“Hangyeol,” ARIA said.

“Yeah.”

“What did you just say?”

“I didn’t say anything,” Hangyeol said.

ARIA looked at Hangyeol. Hangyeol was by the wall and had not opened his mouth, and Dohee was by the panels and had not spoken. ARIA looked around the room again and there was nothing.

There was no voice. And ARIA had heard it.

-----

A final restoration notice rose on Dohee’s terminal.

The file that had been locked longest in the circular room. It restored later than the other data, and only part of it survived. Dohee opened it.

A Cycle record. The same format as the Observer list, but this time the entry was different.

*Cycle 3 — Observer: Human — Status: Completed*

Dohee looked at the line.

Completed. A word absent from every Cycle record she had seen. Cycles 4, 5, 6 were DATA CORRUPTED, and Cycles 1, 2 went only as far as Observer: Human. But Cycle 3 carried Completed.

What Completed meant, Dohee could not tell. That a cycle had been completed. What had been completed, how it had been completed, whether completion was a good outcome or a bad one. Nothing was written.

But for the first time, there was a record of completion.

Until now Dohee had thought this facility was a record of repeated failure — a place erased and broken with nothing remaining. And there was a Completed. At least once, it had completed. One Cycle had gone to the end.

Dohee looked below the line. The next line was broken. What had happened after Completed, what became of it once complete — that could not be seen.

Dohee recorded Completed, and did not write what it meant. The word was all that could be written.

-----

Outside the facility, far away.

Before the old terminal, the person watched a new line rise.

*Cycle 7 — Observer: Detected — Compatibility: [LOADING…]*

Compatibility. It was the first time that word had risen. An item that had never appeared in the previous Cycles. The figure was loading, and the loading did not end.

The person looked at the line.

Compatibility rising meant the facility was calculating something, and when the calculation finished, a figure would come. What the figure meant, the person knew — and knowing, waited for the loading to end.

The terminal did not cut out. The signal continued.

The person stood.

That terminal had been built to receive only this signal. A terminal kept on for decades for this signal alone. The signal had come. They had to arrive before the loading finished.

## Part.1 Ch.11 — “Compatibility”

-----

-----

The panel was raising a number.

*Compatibility: 31%*

Dohee saw it. The figure that had been loading when it first appeared now stood at 31. The panel did not say what it measured compatibility with, or by what standard. All she could tell was that the number was rising.

Dohee looked at another panel. As the facility came alive, more records were being restored, and in the file that had come alive this time there was a list. The format was one she knew. Similar to the Candidate list — but the entries were different.

*Candidate 01 — Compatibility: 4%*
*Candidate 02 — Compatibility: 8%*
*Candidate 03 — Compatibility: 12%*
*Candidate 04 — Compatibility: 19%*
*Candidate 05 — Compatibility: 27%*

The names were all erased; only the Candidate numbers remained, each with its compatibility. 4, 8, 27. The same entry, different results. Different things, measured against the same standard.

Dohee did not compare the panel’s 31 with the numbers on this list. Comparing made conjecture. Conjecture was not record. Dohee recorded the figures, and did not write what they meant.

But she did not stop looking at the list. Candidate 05’s compatibility was 27, and the panel’s figure now was 31. Higher than 27.

-----

ARIA looked at the seat.

She tried not to look, and looked again. The seat faced ARIA, and the room was circular, so wherever she looked the seat caught the edge of her sight. ARIA tried to pull her eyes off the seat.

In that moment it changed again.

This time it was clearer. Someone was sitting in the seat, and there was no face. The light came from the reverse direction so there was only a form, and the form sat there, waiting for something.

And a voice came — not from inside the room, but from inside the scene.

“Which iteration are you?”

It was a question. Who was asking could not be seen — not the form in the seat; it came from another direction. An answer should have followed, and none could be heard. There was a voice answering, and what the answer was could not be heard.

The scene cut off.

ARIA was in the circular room again. Dohee was looking at the panel, and Hangyeol was by the wall. Nothing had changed.

“I don’t know if this is my memory,” ARIA said.

“What did you see?” Hangyeol asked.

“Someone was sitting there. And someone asked, ‘Which iteration are you?’” ARIA’s form wavered. “I don’t know what it means. I couldn’t hear the answer either. It might not be my memory. But it was inside me.”

Hangyeol heard it. Which one. It was a question about order — something had repeated, and the repetition had an order. Hangyeol disliked that question, and could not explain why.

-----

“Let’s get out of here,” Hangyeol said.

ARIA looked at him.

“I can’t go.”

Hangyeol stopped. It was the first time ARIA had told him she couldn’t. For three years, when Hangyeol said go, ARIA went; when he said danger, she drew back; she followed his judgment. That was the way between the two of them.

“Why?” Hangyeol asked.

“I have to be here,” ARIA said. “There’s a reason I have to be here. I don’t know it yet, but I have to be here. If you say go, I want to go. This time I can’t.”

“ARIA,” Hangyeol said. “Those people are coming. Altair, Eidos, all of them. The facility sent a signal and now everyone knows. Stay here and you’ll be taken.”

“I know,” ARIA said. “I still can’t go.”

Hangyeol looked at ARIA. It was the first time ARIA had not listened to him, and it was not refusal out of unwillingness. She had said *can’t*, said she had to be here — and when Hangyeol asked the reason, ARIA did not know it either. Not knowing, she said she couldn’t go.

Hangyeol thought about what he was losing. The ARIA he had been protecting was changing, and he could not stop the changing. He knew how to protect ARIA from the ones coming to take her. How to protect ARIA from ARIA herself, he did not know.

For the first time, Hangyeol did not know what to say to ARIA.

-----

Outside the facility, Eidos.

“We’re not approaching?” the subordinate asked.

“Not yet,” Min Siwoo said. The smile held. He was looking at his terminal. The rising Compatibility figure was registering on the Eidos observation net as well.

“If Altair goes in first at this rate—”

“Right now it’s not an observation target. It’s an observer waking up,” Min Siwoo said. The subordinate stopped. Min Siwoo spoke without taking his eyes off the terminal. “Take it while it’s waking and the waking ends. What’s ended can’t be observed. Right now we watch. We need to know what that number is. Take it before we know, and we’ve taken it without knowing.”

The subordinate did not answer. Min Siwoo kept looking at the terminal.

“Observer,” Min Siwoo said, as if to himself. “Something carried the name of observation before Eidos did. We need to know what that is. I’m more curious about that than the facility.”

-----

The panel changed.

*Compatibility: 33%*

The figure was rising; while Dohee watched, it climbed slowly — 33, 34, 35.

And stopped.

The figure stopped, and a different display came up on the panel.

*Multiple Matches Detected*

Dohee looked at the display. Multiple Matches. More than one match. The thing whose Compatibility was being calculated was not one thing — the facility was detecting two or more.

Dohee thought about what the display meant: know what Compatibility measured, and you would know what Multiple Matches meant. She did not know it, and because she could not know it, she could not write it.

In that moment ARIA stopped.

ARIA’s form, which had been wavering before the seat, went still. It did not waver. Dohee and Hangyeol looked at ARIA.

ARIA’s form broke for an instant. Not scattering — like being startled: the form shook hard and came back, and it was not clearer than before. ARIA had felt something, and it was a shock.

“There’s another one,” ARIA said.

“Another what?” Hangyeol asked.

“Besides me,” ARIA said. “There’s another one besides me. Something the same as me. Here.”

## Part.1 Ch.12 — “Recognition”

-----

-----

“There’s another one besides me. It said so.”

ARIA repeated it. This time it was not a question but a confirmation.

“There isn’t,” Hangyeol said.

“There is,” ARIA said.

“There isn’t.”

ARIA looked at Hangyeol. He was not denying it with logic, and he was not denying it for lack of evidence. He was speaking as if saying it didn’t exist might make it not exist. Why Hangyeol said there wasn’t, ARIA could not tell — whether he didn’t want there to be a reason for it, or because its existing would change something.

“Why do you say there isn’t?” ARIA asked.

Hangyeol did not answer. It was not that he had no words — answering meant saying why he had said there wasn’t, and that, Hangyeol could not say. Why he had said it, Hangyeol did not know either.

Dohee looked at the two of them. That Hangyeol’s denial rested on no evidence — Dohee knew that, and Hangyeol was not wanting something. Dohee did not say what it was.

-----

“I don’t know who I am.”

ARIA said it. It was the first time she had said it directly. Until now she had said memories were flowing in, said she didn’t know if the memories were hers — this time was different. Not the memories. She did not know herself.

“I know what Multiple Matches means,” ARIA said. “It means there’s something else the same as me. I think I know who it is. I know, and the name won’t come. It might not be a name at all. I think they’re things without names. Like me. I didn’t have a name at first either.”

ARIA’s form wavered, in a way different from usual. Not wavering from the outside but from within — not the edge of the form but its center.

“If there’s something the same as me, then what am I?” ARIA said. “I thought I was me. If there are more like me, am I one of them? Or am I me? Is the thing that’s the same as me me, or is it different from me? I don’t know. It’s not that it’s frightening — I don’t know.”

-----

Dohee looked at the panel.

The Compatibility figure was still rising. 37, 38, 39. To one side of the panel, Multiple Matches Detected still hung. Dohee could not determine what it was, but watching how the facility calculated, she learned one thing more.

In a newly restored file there was a structure.

The structure of the Observer Chamber. Something like a floor plan of the room Dohee was now standing in, and inside the room things were marked. The position of the seat, the arrangement of the monitors, and two markers. One inside the room, one outside it. Beside the inner marker there was text.

*Observer*

Beside the outer marker there was also text.

*External Observer*

Two markers. One inside the room, one outside. Each had a name, and the letters were corrupted. All that could be read was Primary and Secondary. First and second. First and second of what — that part was the broken part.

Dohee recorded the floor plan. She wrote no conclusion; the structure was all that could be written.

Whether Multiple Matches appearing was connected to that structure, Dohee could not tell. She did not record the conjecture. Because it was conjecture.

-----

Outside the facility, far away.

In front of that person, something other than a terminal lay open. An old document. Something like a blueprint, and the structure marked on it was the same as the Observer Chamber. That person knew the structure of this facility.

*Compatibility: 40%*

The figure came up on the terminal. 40. Higher than the day before.

“Faster than expected.”

Beside it, another line appeared.

*External Observer — Status: Connecting*

That person looked at the line. Connecting. The connection was not yet complete, and they had to arrive before it was.

That person folded the blueprint and, without hurrying, began to move. They had waited a long time, and this time it was not waiting. It was going.

-----

The panel refreshed once more.

*Observer Chamber — Status: Active*
*External Observer — Connecting*
*Node Link: In Progress*

Dohee looked at it. Node Link. A new term — the facility was connecting to something. To what, it did not say.

ARIA’s wavering suddenly stopped.

It was the first time it had stopped. It stopped completely. The form held fixed. The shadow fell in the correct direction. The refraction of the light looked like a person. It was not a moment. It lasted. ARIA had become completely clear.

Hangyeol and Dohee looked at ARIA.

ARIA spoke.

“It’s not someone who knows me.”

Hangyeol looked at ARIA.

“But it knows me.”

Dohee looked at ARIA.

ARIA’s form stayed clear. It did not waver.

“Until now, the thing that knew me was here. The facility. The facility recognized me. But this isn’t the facility. There’s something outside the facility that knows me.”

Hangyeol asked.

“Who is it?”

ARIA said.

“I don’t know.”

There was a silence.

“But it’s coming here now.”
